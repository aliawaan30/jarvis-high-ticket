# From no idea to a sellable AI offer

Status: proposed course structure, 2 November 2026. Research-informed design, not a completed student or Base44 test. This is teaching support for offer exploration; the offer-creation skill still returns exactly six lines.

## What the student is trying to achieve

Choose one buyer group, understand one problem worth paying to solve, show a credible solution, and prepare an offer they can explain and fulfil. They do not need to master ten services or invent new technology. The module ends with one offer and a practical first buyer list, not a market-research essay.

## Two routes, one destination

**Known-service route:** explore the supplied examples, current advertising or an outside service → match one service to a buyer with the relevant problem → research and demonstrate the adaptation → create the offer.

**AI-audit route:** choose a reachable business type → attract owners with a useful AI opportunity audit → inspect their actual workflows → select one worthwhile improvement → show the proposed solution and agree a scoped implementation. Their broad desire to use AI can start a conversation; it does not establish which build they need.

Blake clarified that this is one funnel for a niche AI automation agency, not a separate fixed automation product. The agency serves one industry across suitable projects and may acquire clients through audits, demonstrations, referrals or other campaigns. Another explicitly supported model is an AI content agency: planning/scripts, supplied footage, AI-assisted editing, review, revisions and approved content delivery.

Do not force custom clients into a preselected template. Do not force known-service clients through a company-wide transformation audit. The deep custom opportunity finder remains available for specialised workflows; it is not every beginner's required starting task.

## Five short lessons and the work each produces

| Lesson | Explain simply | Student/Jarvis exercise | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. What businesses pay for | More paid work, less expensive repetition, fewer delays/errors or more useful capacity. AI is how part of the work gets done. | Walk through receptionist, recovery and proposal-preparation examples, including what the human still does. | Student chooses a known-service or audit-led starting route. |
| 2. Find a buyer with the problem | A niche is a recognisable group with a similar workflow and buying reason. It need not be obscure. | Compare up to three buyer groups; inspect a small named sample, a relevant provider and their existing software. | One buyer group, a first list of roughly 10 real accounts and a reason those accounts may fit. This sample is not a TAM estimate. |
| 3. Understand and explain the fix | Before, after, what gets installed, who uses it, and why the result matters. | Known-service route: prepare a buyer problem hypothesis and confirm it in conversations. Audit route: walk through a real task with the buyer and obtain examples. Separate observations from guesses. | One plain-English offer brief. An early draft can precede calls; it is not a buyer-confirmed diagnosis. |
| 4. Prove the proposed workflow | A demonstration shows what happens. Delivery planning establishes whether it can work with the actual client's tools. | Build one bounded example with synthetic/authorised data. Check access, native alternatives, build effort and support costs with the delivery route available. | A demo and a feasible first implementation; any unverified integration stays explicit. |
| 5. Make the commercial offer | Service description answers what it does; the offer explains why this buyer should act. | Use offer creation for niche, outcome, timeframe, risk reversal, proof and mechanism. Choose a primary acquisition route and coordinated follow-up for the first account list. | Six-line offer, buyer-facing opening, demo link and a clear next action. Public pricing stays out. |

Lesson 5's public opening and supporting delivery work are course exercises outside the offer-creation skill's six-line response. Do not expand that skill into a report.

## Finding niches: enough research to take the next step

For a known service, ask which businesses have the required situation: call volume, paid enquiries, an eligible old-lead database, repeated proposals, support volume, or repeated client onboarding. Look for reachable owners/managers and an ability to benefit financially. Existing spend, staff time and provider deployments can support the hypothesis. Websites and job ads supply clues, not proof of internal pain.

Compare the current software's native solution with the proposed service. The agency can add value through setup, integration, better workflow, training and ongoing management; it does not need a new algorithm. If the native feature already solves the entire problem cheaply, adjust the service or choose another buyer.

Use a small real account sample to check whether buyers can be found. A concentrated B2B audience often suits targeted calls, email and LinkedIn. A larger recognisable audience with a clear offer can support ads. Teach the coordinated campaign in acquisition lessons; do not require a prior closed deal before advertising. Channel access and applicable contact rules belong to campaign setup, not an assumption that Jarvis can automatically message anyone.

## The free AI Opportunity Audit

Front-end message: “Know AI could help your business, but not sure where to start? We’ll walk through how your team works, identify the most useful opportunities and show you what to implement first.”

The free first conversation is a focused diagnosis, not an unpaid enterprise consultancy. Ask:

1. Show me the last time this task happened, from start to finish.
2. How often does it happen, who does it, and how long does it take?
3. Where do people wait, repeat work, make mistakes or lose a sale?
4. Which tools and records are involved? What have you already tried?
5. What would improve if this worked better, and how would we measure it?
6. Who would use the solution and approve the project?

Return the best opportunities, their likely value using the buyer's inputs, and one recommended first project. If figures or access are unknown, show the question that needs answering. A larger paid assessment can be scoped when the business needs one.

Call one: diagnose and agree the problem. Between calls: feasibility, estimated delivery cost, tailored demo and proposal. Call two: review the proposed build, timeline, investment and decision. Advanced delivery details can follow an accepted proposal; basic feasibility must precede promises and pricing.

## The one-page offer brief

Keep student understanding in a shared brief separate from the short marketing offer:

- Buyer and buying situation.
- Problem in their words, current process and financial/time impact.
- Proposed before/after workflow and the human's responsibility.
- Initial scope, delivery route, tooling/access and ongoing support.
- Evidence, demo, material unknowns and existing alternatives.
- Proposed commercial terms and value calculation; approval status of targets and guarantee.
- Six-line offer and first acquisition route.

Use a consistent worked recovery example, then show one audit-led admin workflow so students understand that both revenue and operational improvements can be sold. An illustrative model is not customer proof. Do not make a niche or platform mandatory because it appears in the example.

## Finish line

Ask the student to explain, without reading: “Who buys this? What is happening today? What does it cost them? What changes? What do we actually install? What still needs a person? Why buy from us? How will we find the first prospects?”

If the answer is unclear, repair that part of the brief. If it is clear and delivery is credible, move to acquisition. The module is not finished merely because Jarvis produced six persuasive lines; it is not held open until the student knows every possible service either.
