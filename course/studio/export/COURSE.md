## What is an offer?
Your service is the work you do. Your offer tells the customer what they get, when they get it and why they should choose you.

> EXAMPLE: **The service:** Install an AI receptionist.

**The offer:** We’ll get your plumbing business 5 extra jobs in 30 days with an AI receptionist that answers your missed calls, or we work for free until we do.

The owner wants more jobs. The AI receptionist is how you help them get those jobs. The offer gives them a specific result, a timeframe and a guarantee.

An irresistible offer brings together a result the buyer wants, a believable way to achieve it, a clear timeframe and a guarantee you can stand behind.

## Understand what the problem costs
Before you decide what to sell or charge, understand how much the problem matters to the business.

An owner might say, “We miss a few calls at night.” That sounds small. But what are those missed calls worth?

### Example: missed calls at a plumbing business

> EXAMPLE: The business misses **30 new customer calls a month**.

If it usually turns **20% of those enquiries into jobs**, that is **6 potential jobs**.

At an average of **$2,500 per job**, that is **$15,000 in potential work each month**.

Now you can explain the value of the service. You are helping them win work they could be losing when nobody answers. In a survey of 1,000 US consumers, **82% said they would call a competitor if a business did not answer**. A missed call can become someone else’s job. CallRail, September 2025.

Before a sales call, ask Jarvis to research your niche and service and estimate the size of the problem. It can find typical job values and relevant industry research, then show a rough calculation and the questions to ask the owner.

```
Use the offer menu skill to research what this problem could cost a business in my niche. Find relevant sources, show a rough low, middle and high estimate, and give me the questions to check the numbers with the owner.

```
Use the owner’s numbers: how many genuine customer enquiries they miss, how many normally become jobs and what an average job is worth. The figures above show the calculation; not every missed call will become a sale.

### How this helps you create and price the offer
A problem costing $500 a month supports a different service and price from one costing $15,000 a month. Understanding the cost helps you decide whether the opportunity is worth pursuing and what the client could reasonably pay to fix it.

The $15,000 is potential sales revenue, not profit. You still need to understand the cost of doing those jobs, what your system can recover and what it costs you to deliver.

For an admin task, ask how many hours it takes and what that time costs. For slow quotes, ask how many customers are lost while waiting. The question stays the same: **what does this problem cost the business?**

You will use this again in the sales module, where you work through the numbers with the owner. Here, it helps you choose a worthwhile problem to build your offer around.

## Explain the offer from the buyer’s starting point
Two owners can have the same problem but understand it differently. One knows missed calls are costing jobs. Another thinks customers will just leave a message.

This is buyer awareness: **what they already know about the problem and the ways to solve it.** It changes what you need to explain in your offer and marketing.

![Eugene Schwartz’s five stages of awareness. Start with what the buyer already understands.](/data/img/m2-the-five-stages-of-awareness-ladder.svg)

### Using the AI receptionist example
- **They have not noticed the problem.** Help them see how many enquiries go unanswered and what those could be worth.
- **They know the problem.** Explain how an AI receptionist answers those calls and helps book suitable jobs.
- **They know solutions exist.** Explain what your service handles: setup, their booking rules, calendar connection and ongoing support.
- **They are comparing providers.** Show your demo, relevant results and what is included.
- **They are ready to choose.** Make the timeframe, guarantee and next step clear.

You do not need to memorise these stages. Remember this: if the owner does not understand the problem yet, explain it before expecting them to want your solution. If they already understand it, spend less time explaining the basics and more time showing why your offer fits.

## 1. AI receptionist
**What you sell.** An AI phone agent that answers when the team cannot, takes the caller’s details and books suitable jobs or appointments. The business keeps its number. Missed and after-hours calls forward to the agent, which checks the calendar and sends the team a summary.

**Who to sell it to.** Any business that takes customer calls. Think trades, clinics, salons and other local businesses. Start with one type so the questions, bookings and demo are easy to understand.

See a provider offering AI call and triage agents

### How it changes for each client
Some businesses only need the caller’s details and a message sent to the team. Others need appointment booking, call transfers, answers to routine questions or a new job created in their CRM. A webhook is a message from one system to another, such as telling the CRM to create that job after a call. Agree the actions, booking rules, connections and when a person takes over. A note-taking agent does not, by itself, deliver a promise of extra booked jobs.

**What to show.** A caller rings the demo number, asks about a service and gets the correct next step. Show a booking and a question that needs a human.

> EXAMPLE: We help local trade businesses turn missed calls into booked jobs with an AI receptionist that answers when their team cannot.

## 2. AI appointment-setting agency

**What you sell.** You install and manage the follow-up that turns enquiries into booked consultations. The client gets the system set up around their service, connected to their calendar and looked after by you. They are paying for the work and the result, not just a software login.

**Who to sell it to.** Start with one niche where consultations lead to valuable sales. The business needs enough enquiries and staff available to take the appointments. If you also generate the enquiries with ads, that is a bigger service to deliver.

**A live agency offer.** SyncPro Media sells a dental-implant system combining Meta ads, AI follow-up and consultation booking. Its page advertises a $40K sales target in 40 days with continued free work if missed. Study how it connects the service to a result the buyer wants. Your own promise must fit what you can deliver.

### How it changes for each client
One clinic might need three questions and a booking link. Another needs follow-up across several lead sources, different calendars and a handoff for pricing questions. Agree which enquiries to handle, what makes someone suitable, when to follow up and what counts as a booked consultation.

**What to show.** Submit a test enquiry. Show the follow-up, qualification questions and appointment reaching the right calendar.

> EXAMPLE: We build and run the follow-up system that turns your dental enquiries into booked implant consultations, so your team can focus on treatment sales.

Understand what the client buys, who needs it and what you would show them.

## 3. Old-lead and unsold-quote recovery
**What you sell.** Reconnect with people who enquired or received a quote but never bought. The system follows up, handles agreed questions and brings interested buyers back to the sales team. It tracks which opportunities become paid work.

**Who to sell it to.** Businesses with a useful old database or unsold quotes. Roofers, builders, clinics and home-improvement companies often already have the opportunity sitting in their CRM.

**An agency selling it.** Mixed Media Ventures provides managed database-reactivation campaigns for clients and reseller agencies. Its roofing case reports 127 qualified appointments over 90 days. These are the agency’s reported figures.

### How it changes for each client
You might follow up unsold quotes, older enquiries or previous customers. These need different messages and timing. Agree which records can be contacted, what the system can say, when a sales rep takes over and how the eventual sale is tracked.

**What to show.** Show an eligible old enquiry, the follow-up conversation and the sales-team handoff. Then show where the eventual sale would be recorded.

> EXAMPLE: We’ll get your roofing company 5 more jobs in 30 days from unsold estimates, or we work for free.

## 4. Outbound appointment-setting agency

**What you sell.** You find suitable prospects, write and run the outreach, manage replies and book qualified sales conversations for the client. You build the system in their accounts and keep improving it while their team handles sales.

**Who to sell it to.** A B2B business with a clear offer and someone able to take sales calls. Start with your chosen niche, then check whether there are enough reachable buyers and whether a sale is worth the cost of getting the meeting.

**A live agency offer and client example.** ColdIQ’s managed implementation builds and runs outbound in accounts the client owns. Its Hemlane case explains targeting active rental-property owners and reports a 5.5% reply rate. The result is reported by the agency.

### How it changes for each client
The buyer list, channels, messages and qualification rules depend on the client’s offer and audience. Some need email only; others need email, LinkedIn and human calls working together. Agree what counts as a qualified meeting and who handles replies and sales.

**What to show.** Show the target account, relevant outreach, a reply and the handoff to the client’s sales calendar. Finding contacts alone is not the finished service.

> EXAMPLE: We find your ideal customers, start the conversations and book qualified sales calls for your team.

## 5. Custom AI CRM
**What you sell.** Build a CRM around how the business actually works. Enquiries, quotes, jobs, documents and follow-ups sit together, with agreed tasks automated. Decide what it replaces and what it connects to before you quote.

**Who to sell it to.** Established businesses whose team keeps copying information between spreadsheets, inboxes and software. The owner wants fewer things falling through the cracks and a clear view of each job.

### How it changes for each client
A builder might need quotes, job progress and invoices. A training provider might need enrolments, documents and student records. Map how the team works, then agree what you will replace, what you will connect and what each person can access. Do not promise to replace every tool without checking it.

**What to show.** Show one job moving from enquiry to quote and delivery in the same record. Show who updates it and what the team no longer needs to copy.

> EXAMPLE: We build one CRM around your business so your team stops re-entering information and chasing job updates.

**What you sell.** Turn the client’s footage and ideas into a regular supply of edited reels, captions and other content. AI helps with scripts, cuts and captions. You check the edits and send a consistent batch for approval.

**What you sell.** Choose a type of business and offer a free AI audit. Find where the team loses time, money or opportunities. Recommend the most valuable first fix, show what it would do and sell the implementation. The audit opens the conversation; the build is the paid service.

**Who to sell it to.** Owners who know AI could help but do not know what to implement. Speak to one business type so each conversation teaches you more about the same workflows.

**How you find the paid project.** Have the owner show you a real workflow: what starts it, who handles it, where they copy information and what happens when it gets delayed. ### Two ways to start
**You already like a service:** look for a niche where that service solves a valuable problem. Compare how other agencies serve those businesses and whether there is a gap in the location, customer type or service they provide.

**You already know a niche:** start there. For a custom automation agency, speak to owners about how their business works before deciding what to build. An AI audit is a conversation where you find useful improvements, then recommend and quote the specific project.

### What makes a niche a good fit?
Look for businesses that can gain enough from the result to pay for your service. A business selling valuable jobs, or handling lots of customers, may have more to gain from a small improvement.

Your knowledge of the industry also helps. Do you understand the work? Can you explain the problem in their language? Is there a clear reason they would want it fixed?

Fewer competing providers can create an opening, but it only matters if customers want the service. Choose the service and niche together, then check the problem with real businesses. Staff count alone does not tell you what a business can afford.

## Why ads are so powerful
Outreach is one to one. You call an owner, you have the conversation, you move to the next one. Ads are one to many. You say the message once, on camera, and Meta puts it in front of every business owner it can find who looks like your buyer. In every city you choose. While you sleep.

Ads are not cheap or easy at the start. They are the most consistent way to scale, because when a message works you turn the budget up without finding more hours in your week.

## The whole game in one line
Test as many angles as possible and find as many winners as possible. An angle is one way of talking about your offer: the problem costing the owner money, the result they want, the objection already in their head. Put each one in front of real people, the market tells you which ones land, and you make more of those.

![Test angles. Find the winner. Make versions of the winner. Repeat.](/data/img/m4-angle-to-variations.svg)

## The words do the targeting now
Ads used to be about the settings: ages, interests, job titles, cities. Meta's new ad system changed that. What you say in your video is what finds the right person. Meta watches who stops scrolling, who keeps watching, who clicks, and goes and finds more people like them.

Open with "if you run a plumbing business with more than five vans and your office is drowning in quotes" and Meta finds plumbers with five vans and a drowning office. Open with "AI is changing everything" and Meta finds people who like AI videos, and none of them buy a five figure system. The thing that decides your results is the thing you control: the words, and who they are aimed at.

## Be specific or it does not work
The more precisely your ad names the owner and the pain, the cheaper every number in your account gets. Your niche, their problem, their words: that is your ad, said out loud to a stranger who gives you eight seconds.

## What a customer can cost you
If you can acquire a customer for less than you get paid, it works. That is the floor. The number is CAC, cost to acquire a customer: all the ad spend it took, divided by the number of clients who paid.

At the very least I want you at a 50 percent CAC. Sell for $5,000, spend $2,500 to get that client. That is a pretty bad CAC, but it still works and it does not break you while you learn. Never run a number you cannot afford to lose while an ad is still being tested.

It also depends on your ticket. At $35,000 you can afford a far higher cost per click, lead and booked call than at $1,000, because one close pays for a lot of misses. The trade is a smaller pool of people who can pay it.

![Higher ticket, higher affordable cost per call, smaller pool.](/data/img/m4-cac-vs-ticket.svg)

## Your four numbers
Work backwards from your price, before you spend a dollar. Say you sell at $8,000 and you want CAC under 25 percent. That gives you $2,000 per client won. Close one in four qualified calls and each booked call can cost up to $500. One in three leads books a call, so each lead can cost up to about $165. Made up ratios to show the shape; yours come from your own calls, and your first guesses are fine.

| Your price | $8,000 |  | 
| Most you will spend to win one client | $2,000 |  |

## The numbers inside Meta
The ones that matter, in the order the buyer moves through them.

| Number | What it means | What moves it | 
| CPM | What Meta charges to show your ad a thousand times. | Who you are reaching. Mostly out of your hands. | 
| CTR | Of the people who saw it, how many clicked. | The hook. First three seconds, first line of text. | 
| CPC | What each click costs. | Fix the hook and this falls on its own. | 
| Cost per lead | What one person giving you their details costs. | The match between the ad and the page. | 
| Cost per qualified call booked | What one real buyer on your calendar costs. | Your qualifying questions and booking flow. Plan your budget around this one. | 
| Show rate | Of the calls booked, how many turned up. | Your follow up. Below 60 percent, fix that before you touch the ads. | 
| Close rate | Of the calls that happened, how many bought. | Your sales process. Ads cannot fix a weak call. | 
| CAC | Total spend divided by clients won. | Everything above. The only number that decides whether ads are working. | Half of these are not ad numbers. Show rate and close rate live in your funnel and on your calls. Cheap clicks with a weak funnel behind them is how most people lose money on Meta. Track the whole line, click to cash.

## One winner, many versions
Once you find a winning angle, do not go hunting for a new idea. Make versions of it: different opening line, different setting, same words as a static image. Every ad wears out and a fresh version resets the cost. My rhythm: test three to five angles, find one winner, make five versions, run those while you test the next batch.

## Scale, fix or kill
Give each ad the cost of one or two booked calls before you judge it, and never on its first day. Then it goes one of three ways.

- **Scale.** Booking qualified calls at or under target. Raise the budget around 20 percent every two or three days. Make versions. Do not touch the ad itself.
- **Fix.** One number is broken, the rest are fine. Clicks but expensive leads: the page does not match the ad. Leads but no bookings: your questions or booking flow. Bookings but no shows: the follow up. Fix that one step. Do not rewrite the ad.
- **Kill.** Fair spend, cost per qualified call well over target, or nobody clicking. Turn it off and write down why in one line. A dead ad with a reason is data. A dead ad you keep running out of hope is a bill.

Log every decision in your CRM: ad, outcome, reason, next action. In a month that record tells you exactly what this market responds to.

## The call structure that has closed $22k+ deals

## The doctor frame

Think about how a specialist runs an appointment. You booked in because something hurts. They do not thank you for turning up and they do not quote a surgery fee before they have examined you. They ask what is wrong, how long, what you have tried. Then they come back with a plan.

That is the frame. They came to you for the fix. Diagnose, then prescribe. If you believe you are lucky to be on the call, every question sounds like permission seeking and every number sounds like an apology. If you believe you are the doctor, the same words land completely differently.

### m6.2 Call One: Find the Pain

*The whole job of call one is to understand their business well enough to put a number on what is broken, and hear them agree to it. Here is the script.*

**They leave with:** The full call one script, in order.

This is the call that decides the deal. Not because you close here, you usually do not, but because everything you say later is built from what you learn here. Run it well and the second call writes itself. Run it badly and no script on earth saves it.

Here is the whole call, in the order I run it, with the words I say. Keep it open next to you for your first ten calls. After ten you will not need it.

![Six steps. The only output that matters is step five: a number they agreed to out loud.](/data/img/m6-call-one-flow.svg)

## Before you say a word

You are the prize. They booked into your calendar because something you said hit a pain and they want it fixed. So you do not open with "thank you for jumping on today". That line makes them the chooser and you the applicant. Warm, curious, unhurried. Never grateful. And remember the rule that runs the whole call: the one asking the questions is in control. You ask. They talk.

## Step 1. The open

Thirty seconds of human, then straight into the one question. No agenda, no company story.

> SAY THIS: Hey [name], good to meet you properly. How's the week going?

[Let them answer. One line back. Then:]

So [name], tell me, was there anything specific you're having problems with currently? Walk me through it.

**Before you move on:** you know whether they came for one fix or for direction. Most people who booked off an ad or a message name the exact pain you called out. Some say "we know we need to do something with AI, we just don't know where". Both are fine. Same script, you just listen longer on the second one.

## Step 2. Let them tell the story

Get them explaining the problem in their own words, as things that happened, not adjectives. Do not fix anything yet. The moment you say "we can automate that" they stop talking, and what they say next is what you sell them with.

> SAY THIS: Okay, so when you say [their words], what does that actually look like day to day? Take me through the last time it happened.

And who's dealing with it when it happens?

How long has it been like that?

What have you tried so far to fix it?

**Before you move on:** you can describe the problem as a sequence of events, in their words.

If they came for direction rather than one fix, and the business is small enough that one person can describe it, add this. Skip it for a bigger company, the owner of a forty person business will not walk you through everyone's week and it makes you sound junior.

> SAY THIS: Before I suggest anything I want to understand how the business actually runs. Where do customers come from, and what happens from the moment one gets in touch to the moment they've paid? Where does it get messy?

## Step 3. What they have now

Something is already in the gap. A person, a system, a spreadsheet, a receptionist who goes home at five. The current fix is always part of the cost.

> SAY THIS: And right now, what's handling that? Is it a person, a system, a bit of both?

What does that cost you? Not just the subscription, the whole thing: the person, the time, the stuff that slips through.

**Before you move on:** you know the tools, the people, the workarounds, and roughly what each costs.

## Step 4. The four questions that find the number

This is the core of the call. Everything before it was warming up. Four questions, in this order, turn a complaint into a dollar figure they will agree to.

- **What is actually happening?** The problem as events.
- **What are you doing about it right now?** Tools, people, workarounds, nothing.
- **Who does that work?** Names, roles, hours. This is where wages enter.
- **What is it costing you?** Wages, software, lost jobs. Everything converts to dollars.

> SAY THIS: Let me make sure I've got this. So right now [restate the problem as events]. To deal with it you've got [the current fix], and [person] spends roughly [hours] a week on it. Is that about right?

Okay. So let's put a number on it. [Person] is costing you what, roughly, a year? And the software? And how many jobs or customers a month do you reckon slip because of this? What's one of those worth?

**Before you move on:** you have the parts: wages, software, lost jobs, each with a rough number.

## Step 5. Say the number and get the yes

Add it up out loud and ask them if it is right. You want a figure they own, not one you sold them. If they push back, lower it until they agree. A conservative number they said yes to beats an impressive number they rolled their eyes at.

> SAY THIS: So if I add that up, this is costing you somewhere around [number] a year. Does that sound right to you, or am I off?

[If they say it's high: Fair enough, what would you put it at? Then use their number.]

**Before you move on:** they said yes to a number, out loud. If you do not have this, you do not have a call two. Go back to step four.

## Step 6. Book call two, or close now

If your offer is specific and you already know what you are going to build, this is where you say "give me a sec, I'll grab your proposal and we can go through it", then run the call two moves. If it is a custom build, you book call two. Give them the real reason: you need to go and plan it properly with your technical partner. Get it in the diary before you hang up.

> SAY THIS: Okay [name], this has been really useful. Here's what I'm going to do. I need to go back and plan out exactly what fixing this would look like in your business, with my technical partner, with the real numbers. Not a generic deck, the actual thing we'd build for you.

Give me two days. Does Thursday at ten work to walk through it? Twenty minutes.

[Book it on the call. Send the invite while they are still on the line.]

One last thing. Is there anyone else who'd need to be in that conversation if we end up working together? Good, bring them.

**Before you hang up:** call two is in both diaries and the decision makers are named. That last question is what stops "I need to check with my partner" killing the deal next week.

### m6.3 Prescribe the Solution

*They gave you a problem and a number. Now you design the thing that fixes it, check it can be built, and price it against the problem, not the work.*

**They leave with:** A prescribed solution and a price for one real prospect.

Most people in this space price the work. Hours, tokens, licences, a margin on top. Then they wonder why a $4,000 quote gets ghosted and a $22,000 one gets signed in the same week. I price the problem.

## Price the problem, not the work
Know exactly what the problem is costing them and charge against that. Call one gave you the number. If a problem costs them $2k a year and you try to charge $20k, no amount of charm fixes that. If it costs them $5 million a year, $50k for a website is a sensible decision and they know it. The Australian government spent $96 million on a website because the problem was worth that much to them. Price follows problem size.

## The working range
I charge 5 to 10 percent of the problem's annual cost for the build, and I show the running costs separately and honestly. Anchor at 10, land at 5.

| Problem cost per year (agreed on call one) | 10 percent | 5 percent | 
| $60k | $6k | $3k | 
| $150k | $15k | $7.5k | 
| $300k | $30k | $15k | 
| $600k | $60k | $30k | The smaller the problem, the smaller the deal. Below a certain size it is not worth a custom build at all.

## The $22,000 AI CRM

> EXAMPLE: **A high ticket construction company.** Their pain was four things, all found on call one.

**1. Platform limitations.** The CRM they used was rigid. Every change meant going through the platform's support, submitting tickets and asking for new manual things to be added. It felt like a bolt on to the business, not a custom solution.

**2. The cost.** $13k a year in platform costs alone. Their previous CRM also charged per job to send invoices and receive payments, which meant $100 per new customer account.

**3. Manual tasks** done by one employee which could 100 percent be removed. She only worked three days a week but cost them over $65k a year.

**4. Job efficiency, start to completion.** Each job took several weeks. By introducing a few automations into the CRM, they could add an extra four jobs a week with no extra staff.

Their problem was costing them around $110k a year in platform fees, per job charges and wages, plus the extra jobs they could not take with their current stack, probably another $500k a year in revenue, not profit. So when the price was $22k, it was an easy sell.

That is the structure of every good deal. Four pains, each with a number. A cost side and a revenue side. A price that is a small fraction of the cost side alone. The client is not deciding whether to spend $22k. They are deciding whether to keep spending $110k.

## What a prescribed solution looks like
A prescription is not a feature list. It is three things, in order.

- **What gets installed.** The system, named plainly. "A custom CRM with automated invoicing and job scheduling", not "an AI powered operations layer".
- **In what order.** What goes live first, what follows, what waits. First thing live should hit the biggest pain, so they feel the win early.
- **What each piece is worth.** Map each part of the build back to one of the pains and its number. If a piece does not map to a pain, cut it. It is cost with no story.

## Get the real build cost before you price
Take the brief to your technical partner and ask what they will charge to build it fully. Mark up from there. Building it yourself with Jarvis, scope it the same way. If 5 percent of the problem does not clear your build cost with margin, the problem is too small or the solution is too big. Fix one before call two.

## The proposal must show the running costs
Show the real monthly running cost next to the build price: AI usage, subscriptions, whatever it needs. Hiding it feels clever until month two when their card gets charged. On the page it becomes part of the story: "you were paying $13k a year to a platform that would not change, this runs for a fraction of that and it is yours."

By this point you know the buyer’s problem and have checked the proposed solution with the delivery team. This lesson turns that into a document you can walk through on the sales call. The buyer should understand what is wrong, what it costs, what changes, when it happens and what they pay.

## A document about their business
Default to one readable page where the scope fits. If you need a second page or a scope appendix, use it. Don’t squeeze tiny text onto one page, and don’t turn every heading into a slide.

### m6.5 Call Two: Present and Close

*Everything on this call was set up by the last one. Present the proposal properly, speak like the doctor, say the number, then stop talking.*

**They leave with:** The full call two script and the anchor, word for word.

Call two is where the money is, and it is the easier call if call one did its job. You are not persuading anyone. You are presenting a fix for a problem they already told you costs them real money, at a fraction of that money.

If you are in the group or private tier, bring the draft and the call one recording to the weekly session before you present. Closing a specific offer on one call, the same four moves apply, straight after the number.

## How to present the proposal

Four moves, in order. Recap the problem before the solution and price. Open a separate demo when it helps explain the proposed work; return to the short proposal for the terms.

### 1. Recap their words and collect the yeses

Their problem, their number, played back to them. Every sentence ends in a question they can only answer yes to.

> SAY THIS: [Name], good to see you again. Before I show you anything, I want to make sure I've got this right, because if I've misunderstood something the rest of this is pointless.

Last time you told me [the problem as events, their phrase]. Is that still the picture?

And right now that's being handled by [current fix], with [person] spending about [hours] a week on it. Right?

And when we added it up, between [wages], [platform] and [lost jobs], we landed on this costing you around [number] a year. Is that still a fair number?

Okay. So this current system is costing you [number] a year. Good. Let me show you what I'd do about it.

If they revise the number down, use theirs.

### 2. Walk the plan, tied to their problem

Problem, number, what changes. Slowly. Every feature you mention gets attached to a sentence they said on call one. If you cannot attach it, leave it out.

> SAY THIS: So here's what I found. The thing underneath [the problem] is actually [root cause in plain words]. That's why [the current fix] keeps not working: it's built for [what it was built for], not for how you actually run jobs.

What I'd put in is [the system, named plainly]. Here's how a [job, lead, order] moves through it now, and here's how it moves through it after. The difference is [the two or three things that change]. [Person] stops doing [the manual work] and starts doing [the valuable thing].

Does that match how you'd want it to work? Anything in there that wouldn't fit?

### 3. Show the demo

Share your screen. Three to five steps, about a minute. Narrate what the business is doing, not what the software is doing. Make a point of the exception step, that is where they see their own person using it.

> SAY THIS: This is one job going through it. Here's the request coming in. Here's the system doing [the first thing] on its own. Now here's one where it's not sure, see, the [field] is missing, so it flags it and [person] takes ten seconds to fix it instead of an hour to chase it. And here's where it lands in [their system].

This is a mock up, built on your workflow, so you can see the shape. The real one plugs into [their tools].

Can you see [person] using that?

### 4. Anchor, then say the number

Ladder down from the number they agreed to, a yes at each rung. The last rung is your price. Then silence.

![Each rung is a yes. The price arrives as a fraction of a number they already agreed to.](/data/img/m6-anchor-ladder.svg)

> SAY THIS: So [name], let's say I came to you with a solution to your [problem cost] a year problem, and let's say that solution only cost 10 percent of the total problem's cost. As a business owner, does that sound like a viable investment? You pay 10 percent to solve 100 percent of the problem and keep over [90 percent of the number] a year?

[Wait for the yes. If they hedge: it's only a thought exercise, work with me here.]

Well, I agree, 10 percent would be a fair charge to solve this. But I'm not charging you 10 percent. I'm not even charging you 7.5 percent. I'm charging you 5 percent of the total problem's cost to solve it for you, so [price], and it'll be done in [x] days.

[Stop. Do not speak. Let the silence sit until they fill it.]

The same move in dollars, the way I ran it on a $150k problem:

> SAY THIS: So this is costing you about $150k a year. If I could remove that for $50k, would that be a smart business investment? Take $150k of expense off the books for $50k?

[Yeah, I guess.]

Good. I'm not going to charge you $50k. It's $5k to set the whole thing up, and $500 a month for maintenance and any changes after that.

[Silence.]

Anchored at fifty, five is a no brainer.

## How to speak

Three habits.

- **Slow down.** Fast sounds like getting it over with before they object. Slow sounds like someone who has done this before.

- **Silence after the number.** Say the price, then stop. It feels like an hour. It is five seconds. Whoever speaks first moves, and it is not you.

- **Their words, not yours.** If they called it "the office being flat out", so do you. Translate it into your language and it becomes your idea instead of their problem.

## The one asking the questions is in control

When you are presenting it feels like you should be talking. You should not. Every move above ends in a question, because the question makes them say the next step. Three minutes into a monologue, stop and ask something. If they start interrogating you, answer in two sentences and ask a question back. You are the doctor. Doctors ask.

When they frame the price as a cost, do not argue. Ask: "Compared to the [number] a year it's costing you now, where does [price] sit?"

> NOTE: Watch this on reframing before your first call two: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RVbvhPGFi6E&t=177s

## Close and set next steps

When they speak again it is a yes, a question or an objection. A yes goes straight to logistics. A question gets answered, then you ask again. You are not chasing. You are scheduling the treatment.

> SAY THIS: [On a yes:] Great. Here's how it works from here. I'll send the agreement tonight, the deposit of [amount] secures your build slot, and we'll do a one hour onboarding call on [day] where we collect access and the sample data. You'll see the first piece live by [date]. Running costs are in the Costs section, so no surprises on your card. Shall I send the agreement to this email?

[On a question:] Good question. [Answer it in two sentences.] So, with that cleared up, does the plan make sense to you?

[On hesitation with no stated reason:] What's the part you're sitting on? Is it the money, the timing, or whether this actually works in your business?

That last line sorts every vague hesitation into one of three objections.

Payment is the same every time: a deposit to start, the balance at launch, running costs as their own monthly line. Send the agreement the same day.

```
Use the Proposed build out, Timeline and Costs sections of this saved proposal into a one page plain language service agreement for a [country] small business: deliverables, exclusions, what the client provides and when, milestones with dates, [price] paid as [deposit] on signing and the balance at launch, monthly running costs as separate lines, support included for [period], how either side ends it. Flag anything a lawyer should check.

```

Rehearse the anchor out loud with your real numbers, and practise not filling the silence. Hardest five seconds in sales, and where the money is.

### m6.6 Objections

*An objection is not a no. It is the last piece of pain you have not found yet. Identify it, then prescribe.*

**They leave with:** The nine objections and the words to answer each.

Nobody argues a prospect into a deal. Every objection is handled the same way: stay calm, find the real concern underneath, prescribe. When someone hesitates without saying why, sort it.

> SAY THIS: What's the part you're sitting on? Is it the money, the timing, or whether this actually works in your business?

Money, timing or fit. Every objection lives in one of those.

## 1. "It's too expensive"
If they say this after the anchor, the number was never really theirs. Go back to the number.

> SAY THIS: Fair enough. Let me check something, because I'd rather you say no than buy something that doesn't make sense. We agreed this is costing you around [number] a year, right? So the question isn't really whether [price] is a lot. It's whether you'd rather keep paying [number] a year. If the number's wrong, tell me, and we'll re-look at the whole thing.

## 2. "I need to think about it"
Nobody thinks about it. There is an unspoken objection or no urgency. Sort it.

> SAY THIS: Of course. Can I ask, what specifically do you want to think through? Because if it's something I can answer now, I'd rather do that than have you sit with a question for a week. Is it the money, the timing, or whether it'll work for you?

## 3. "I need to check with my partner"
Rare if you asked on call one who else decides. Never let the partner hear a second hand version. Get them on a call.

> SAY THIS: Makes sense, it's their business too. The thing is, if you explain it to them you'll get the half of it you remember, and they'll ask questions you can't answer, and it'll die in the kitchen. Let's do fifteen minutes with both of you, I'll run the short version. When are they free?

## 4. "We tried AI and it didn't work"
Gold. It is almost always a generic tool bolted on, which is exactly what you are not selling.

> SAY THIS: What did you try? [Listen.] Yeah, that's the pattern. Someone plugged a chatbot in and hoped. What we mapped on the last call is different, it's built around how [person] actually runs a job, and you saw the step where it hands the edge cases back to a human instead of guessing. That's the bit the last one didn't have.

## 5. "Just send me the proposal"
A proposal read without you is a price list. It follows call two, it never replaces it.

> SAY THIS: I will, and I'll send it today. But I'll be honest, a proposal on its own is just a document with a number on it, and you'll skip straight to the number. Give me twenty minutes to walk you through it so the number makes sense, then you'll have it in writing to go over in your own time.

## 6. "Not right now, maybe next quarter"
Timing is a cost of delay. You have the annual number. Divide it.

> SAY THIS: Totally get it. Just so we're both looking at the same thing: at [number] a year, next quarter costs you about [number divided by four] in the meantime, and that's before the jobs you can't take. If the timing's genuinely wrong I'll come back when it's right. But if it's just that it's never a good time, this is roughly what waiting costs.

## 7. "What if it breaks?"
A fulfilment question in an objection costume. Answer with the support line and the exception step they saw in the demo.

> SAY THIS: Good question, and it's why the support line is on page five. For [period] after launch I'm the one who fixes it, included. After that it's [monthly retainer], and you saw in the demo it's built to hand things back to [person] rather than guess, so when something's off you know the same minute, not the next month.

## 8. "We could build this ourselves"
Maybe they could. Agree, then ask what the attempt costs. You sell the outcome by a date, not the code.

> SAY THIS: You probably could, honestly, the tools are out there. The question is whether you want [person] learning to build software for the next three months instead of doing their job, and what it costs you if version one doesn't quite work. You're not paying me for the code. You're paying to have it running by [date], done by someone who's done it before, so you can get back to [their actual business].

## 9. "We're too small for this"
If the number is genuinely small, say so and walk away. If it is real, size is irrelevant.

> SAY THIS: Maybe. Let's look at it. The problem's costing you [number] a year. That's not a big company problem or a small company problem, it's just [number] leaving the business. If it were [much smaller number] I'd tell you not to bother and I'd mean it. At [number], it's worth fixing.

## The three you will hear

```

Based on what this specific client said, which three objections from this list are they most likely to raise on call two: too expensive, need to think, check with partner, tried AI before, send me the proposal, not right now, what if it breaks, guarantee, build it ourselves, too small? For each, quote the thing they said that makes you think so, and write a two sentence answer in my voice using their own numbers and words.

```
Say the three that scare you most out loud until they sound like you.

*You have money in the bank and a promise to keep. The first decision is who builds it.*

**They leave with:** A rule for every signed deal: a developer builds it, or you build it with Jarvis.

Once, at the start of the module. Every Jarvis step in this module needs them.

In this module, you’ll learn how to deliver the work you’ve sold, hand it over properly and look after the client afterwards. You can build it yourself with Jarvis or hire a developer and manage the delivery.

You’re doing this so a sale becomes a working service and a client who wants to stay. We’ll cover finding and checking developers, agreeing the build, testing, training the client and ongoing support.

**By the end, you’ll have a delivery plan, a way to check the finished work and a clear process for handover, support and retaining the client.**

The deposit has landed. You owe a business owner a working system, and how you deliver it decides whether this deal makes you money or eats the next three weeks of your life. There are two ways to deliver. Pick one before you touch a single build.

## The two paths

![Both paths start with the same build plan and end at the same handover. The only difference is who builds in the middle.](/data/img/m7-two-paths.svg)

**Arbitrage.** You sold it, a developer builds it. You scope the job, hand them a build plan, pay a fixed price in milestones, and keep the difference between what the client paid you and what the developer cost. Your job is the client, the scope and the money. Their job is the build.

**Self fulfilment.** You sold it and you build it, with Jarvis doing most of the work. Same build plan, handed to Jarvis one piece at a time, tested, delivered by you. Higher margin, more of your hours, and it only works if you are comfortable in the tools.

## My honest recommendation

If you are not technical, bring someone technical in to finish the job. Every time. Do the creative side yourself: the workflow design, the client conversations, the demo. Then hand the build to someone who already knows how to wire it, instead of learning it from scratch while a paying client waits.

That is not a comment on your ability. You just proved you can find a business owner, diagnose a real problem and get paid five figures to fix it. That skill is rare and it is the one that scales. Debugging an integration at eleven at night is not rare, and plenty of good people will do it for a fixed fee. The people who stall here are never the ones who could not sell. They sold, then disappeared into a build they were not ready for.

> NOTE: I keep a list of developers we have already vetted, and the weekly strategy session is there for exactly this. Signed a deal and not sure who should build it? Bring it to the session before you start anything.

## How to decide

Three questions about the deal in front of you. Answer them honestly, not optimistically.

| Question | Hand it to a developer | Build it yourself | 

| Have I built something like this before, end to end, and had someone else use it? | No, or only a demo | Yes, in a live business | 

| Does it plug into tools I have never connected? Their CRM, accounting, booking, phones. | Yes, more than one | No, or one I know | 

| What happens to my pipeline if this build takes three times longer than I think? | Outreach stops | Outreach keeps running without me | 

Two or more in the middle column: hand it off. Two or more on the right: build it with Jarvis. Split: hand it off this time and sit beside the developer, so the next one is yours.

## What stays the same on both paths

- **The build plan comes first.** Nobody builds anything until the scoping skill has turned your call notes and proposal into a full plan. That plan is the contract between what was sold and what gets built.

- **It is built on test data.** The client's live records are never where you find out there is a bug.

- **It is tested the way the client will use it** before they ever see it.

- **Handover is a process**, not a login in an email. Training, sign off, invoice, support window.

## What the margin looks like

> EXAMPLE: Made up numbers, to show the shape. You sell an intake and quoting system for $12,000 with $400 a month running costs. Hand it off: a developer quotes $3,500 fixed in three milestones, you spend ten hours on scoping, reviews and handover, margin $8,500. Build it yourself: no developer cost, forty hours with Jarvis, margin $12,000, about $300 an hour of your time. Both are good deals. The only question is which one you can finish while your outreach keeps running.

### m7.2 Arbitrage: Find and Vet Developers

*Your job is the client and the money. Their job is the build. Getting this bench right is the whole model.*

**They leave with:** Two or three vetted developers, each paid for a test task, with rates agreed.

Handing a build off only works if you have someone to hand it to before you need them. The worst time to find a developer is the week after a deposit lands. Build a bench of two or three people you have already tested, so when a deal closes the build plan goes out the same day.

## Start with my list

I keep a list of developers we have vetted on the kinds of systems you are selling. Ask for it in the group or at the weekly strategy session. Still find one of your own, so you are never waiting on someone else's calendar.

## Where else to look

- **Upwork.** The biggest pool, easy to post to, escrow protects both sides. Most people find their first builder here.

- **Your own network.** Anyone who has had an automation or an app built for a real business. A referral from someone who paid for the work beats fifty profiles.

- **The communities around the tools.** People who answer questions for free in the forums for the client's platform usually build for money too.

## Jarvis writes the job post

A good job post describes the client's business, the specific thing being built and the skills it needs, in that order. "AI automation expert needed" gets two hundred replies from people who have never shipped anything.

```
Write an Upwork job post for a developer to build this system. Here is my offer [paste] and the build plan for this client [paste the plan from the scoping skill, or the proposal if the plan is not done yet]. Structure it as: one paragraph on the client's business and the problem in plain words; a short list of exactly what is being built, including every tool it has to connect to; the specific skills and tools the developer must have already used, named; what I will provide (the full build plan, test data, access); how I want to work (fixed price, three milestones, a short paid test task first, daily written updates); and two questions they must answer in their proposal that prove they read the post. Under 300 words. Do not mention the client's name. Do not use the word expert.

```

## Posting on Upwork

- **Create a client account** under your business name and verify a payment method.

- **Post the job.** Fixed price, not hourly. Budget at the developer cost you priced the deal on, not what the client paid.

- **Set the two screening questions.** Anyone who does not answer them gets deleted unread.

- **Invite three to five profiles** whose completed jobs match the tool stack. Ignore hourly rate and skills lists.

- **Wait 48 hours.** Shortlist three. Reply to nobody else.

## How I vet them

Three things predict whether a developer will deliver. None of them is their profile.

**A small paid test task.** Pay each shortlisted person for one small piece of the actual job you can check in an hour: connect to the test CRM and create a record, parse one sample email into fields. You are watching whether they ask or guess, and whether what comes back works.

**Past work you can see.** One thing a real business uses today, and a two minute recording of it working. Not a portfolio page.

**How they reply.** Inside a working day, short plain sentences, a question when unclear, a screenshot when done. Slow or vague on a paid test will be worse on the real build.

| Signal | Pass | Fail | 

| Test task | Works, on time, one clarifying question asked | Late, guessed, or does not run | 

| Past work | One live system, shown working, similar stack | Screenshots of dashboards, no live use | 

| Communication | Same day, plain words, shows rather than tells | Days between replies, jargon, excuses | 

| Pricing | Fixed price per milestone, happy to do a test | Hourly only, refuses a test, wants 100% up front | 

## Rates and the shape of the deal

Fixed price in three milestones: built on test data, tested and reviewed by you, live and handed over. A third each, released when you have seen it work. Never everything up front. Get three quotes against the same plan and you know the market inside two days. My rule: the developer cost sits well under half of what the client paid for the build. If it does not, the deal was priced wrong, not the developer.

> NOTE: Keep the developer and the client apart. You are the account. Updates go through you, the developer never sees the client's price, the client never sees the developer's quote. That is what keeps the margin.

Post one job this week, even before you close, framed as a paid test task on your offer's core workflow. Two names you have paid and would pay again. That is the bench.

### m7.3 Arbitrage: Scope, Hand Off, Keep the Margin

*A bad handover is how a profitable deal turns into three weeks of unpaid project management.*

**They leave with:** A full build plan from the scoping skill, handed to a developer with milestones, a budget and a date.

You know more about this client's business than anyone about to build for them, and all of it lives in notes, a proposal and your head. Turn it into one document a developer can build from without a single clarifying call. That document protects your margin.

## Why the scope is the whole job

Every expensive build problem is a scoping problem in disguise. The brief said automate intake and never said what happens when the email has no phone number. Nobody wrote down which of their three spreadsheets was the real one. All of it was knowable the day the deal closed. Writing it down then costs an hour. Finding it out later costs the margin.

![One plan, two builders. Everything from the calls goes in, one build plan comes out, and it feeds a developer or Jarvis the same way.](/data/img/m7-scope-to-build.svg)

## The scoping skill

The scoping skill is in your Jarvis pack. Feed it your call one notes and the number you agreed, your call two notes, the proposal as presented, and anything the client sent you. It gives back one document, always in this shape.

| Section | What it must contain | 

| Problem summary | The problem in the client's words, who it lands on, what it costs. | 

| What gets built | One paragraph a business owner would agree with, then the modules, each named in a sentence. | 

| Integrations | Every tool it touches, what it reads and writes, who owns the login. | 

| Data in and out | Each input and output with an example. Which record wins when two disagree. | 

| Edge cases | Missing field, duplicate, uncertain match, person on leave, tool down. Who gets told. | 

| Human steps | Every point where a person approves, chooses or is notified. Named by role. | 

| Milestones | Three: built on test data, tested and reviewed, live and handed over. | 

| Acceptance tests | For each milestone, the checks that mean it is done, written so a non technical person could run them. | 

| Budget split | The developer budget across the three milestones. Nothing about the client price. | 

| Dates | Start, three milestones, go live, and what the client must provide by when. | 

| Unknowns to confirm | Everything the skill could not find in your notes. This goes to the client before the build starts. | 

```
Run the scoping skill on this job. Here are my call one notes and brief [paste], my call two notes [paste], the proposal as presented [paste], and what the client sent me [paste or describe]. Write the full build plan in the standard shape: problem summary, what gets built, modules, integrations, data in and out, edge cases, human steps, milestones, acceptance tests, budget split against a developer budget of [amount], dates from a start of [date], and the unknowns to confirm with the client. Write it for a developer who has never spoken to the client. Do not invent any capability, integration or data that is not in what I gave you. Where you had to guess, put it in the unknowns list instead.

```

## Read it before you send it

Read the unknowns list first. Most are one message to the client: which spreadsheet is current, who approves a big quote, what their booking tool is called. Send it today. Then read the edge cases and decide any the client never mentioned, or the developer decides them for you. Never send a plan with unknowns open. A plan with holes gets a quote with holes, and the holes become change requests at your cost.

## The handoff

- **Send the plan, ask for a fixed quote against the three milestones.** Two working days.

- **Agree price and dates in writing.** Per milestone, released on acceptance. A deposit for milestone one is normal, all of it up front is not.

- **Give them test data and sandbox access**, never the client's live accounts.

- **Set the rhythm.** A short written update every working day, a screen recording at every milestone, questions come to you.

- **Log it against the deal** in your CRM: developer, quote, milestone dates, what has been paid.

> NOTE: You are the account manager, not the messenger. When the developer asks something you do not know, ask the client as if it is your question, because it is. The client bought you.

## Keeping the margin

The margin is lost in three places: scope that grew without a price change, milestones paid before they were checked, and your hours disappearing into chasing. The plan fixes the first. Acceptance tests fix the second: a milestone is paid when the tests pass on a recording you watched. The daily update fixes the third. When the client asks for something not in the plan: "Good idea. It is not in this build, so I will scope it as a follow on once we are live." Scope creep is the next invoice, as long as it never gets built for free.

> EXAMPLE: Made up example. You sell a quoting system to a landscaper. The proposal says quotes come from a site visit form. Your call two notes show half the enquiries come in by text. The scoping skill puts "text message intake: in scope or follow on?" at the top of the unknowns. You ask, it matters, and it becomes a $1,800 follow on instead of an unpaid surprise in week three.

Run the skill, clear every unknown with the client today, send the plan to the two developers on your bench for a fixed quote.

### m7.4 Self Fulfilment: Build It With Jarvis

*The same Jarvis that helped you sell it can build most of it. Twenty small jobs, in order, each checked before the next.*

**They leave with:** The first working version of the client's system, on test data, with a build log.

The build is not one task. It is twenty small ones, done in order, each checked before the next. Jarvis does most of the typing. You do the ordering, the checking and the judgement. The people who struggle here are not short on skill. They asked Jarvis to build the whole system in one message and spent a week untangling what came back.

## Same plan, different builder

You still start with the scoping skill and clear the unknowns with the client first. Skip the developer budget, but give yourself dates. A build with no go live date drifts. Tempted to add something? Is it in the plan? If not, it waits.

## Build the way Jarvis builds best

![One module, one message, one test. Round and round until the last module passes.](/data/img/m7-build-loop.svg)

- **Set up the shell first.** The empty app, the empty tables, the connections to the tools with test credentials. Confirm each connection reads and writes one record before moving on.

- **Build modules in plan order**, which is the order a job moves through the system. Intake before processing, processing before output. Nobody has ever been paid for a dashboard that shows nothing.

- **One module per message.** Paste that module's section of the plan and ask for only that. Run its acceptance test. Pass, move on. Fail, fix that one thing before touching anything else.

- **Edge cases get their own messages.** Happy path first, tested, then each edge case from the plan as its own change, tested on its own.

- **Human steps get built as real steps.** A status a person changes, a message a person receives. Do not let Jarvis quietly automate a decision the plan says a person makes.

### The build message I use every time

```
We are building module [number], [name], from this build plan [paste the plan once at the start of the session, then refer to it]. Build only this module. Here is its section of the plan: [paste]. Use the test data in [where it is]. Do not touch any other module. When it is done, tell me exactly what you built, what you did not build, and how I run the acceptance test for this module from the plan. If anything in the plan is unclear or impossible in this tool, stop and ask me before building.

```

## Going back and forth

Jarvis builds a piece, you test it with a real example, you say what is wrong in plain words, it fixes it. "It doesn't work" produces guesses. "I sent the sample email with no phone number and it created a job with a blank phone field instead of flagging it like the plan says" produces a fix.

- **Describe the input, what happened, and what should have happened.** Every time, in that order.

- **One fix per message.** Three things wrong, three messages. Batching them is how the second fix undoes the first.

- **Ask it to explain before it changes anything big.** "Before you change this, tell me in two sentences what you think is causing it." Half the time you spot the real cause yourself.

> NOTE: Keep the client's live data out of the build. Every record you test on is made up or a sample with real names removed. The first time real data touches the system is the go live at handover, with the client watching.

## When to stop and get help

One integration has eaten more than a couple of hours, the tool cannot do something the plan assumes, or you have fixed the same bug three times: stop. Bring it to the weekly strategy session, or hand that one module to a developer from your bench at a fixed price while you keep building the rest. Most people who have done a few deals do both inside the same one.

> EXAMPLE: Made up example. You build an enquiry triage system for a plumber. Intake and the job record take two evenings with Jarvis. The booking tool needs an API you have never used. You pay a developer $400 fixed to wire that one connection against the plan, and keep building while you wait.

## Track it like a project

Keep a build log in Jarvis against the deal. It becomes the record of what was delivered.

```
Create a build log for the [client] deal from the build plan. One row per module with: built, acceptance test passed, edge cases done, and notes. Update it every time I tell you a module is done or a test has passed. Each time I ask, show me the log and tell me the next module to build in plan order and any client inputs still outstanding.

```

Set up the shell, build module one, run its test, tick the log. The rest is the same loop.

### m7.5 Self Fulfilment: Test It Like the Client Will

*Clients do not use systems the way you built them. Find that out yourself, in private.*

**They leave with:** A tested build with the edge cases handled and a dated test log.

Every module passed its own test. That is not the same as the system working. The client is going to put a real job through it, start to finish, on a busy Tuesday, from their phone, with a typo in the email address. Before they see it, you run it the way they will, end to end, and fix what breaks in private.

## Why I never skip this

The first time a client sees their system is the moment they decide whether you are a professional. If the first job they push through gets stuck, the build could be ninety five percent perfect and they will remember the five. If it runs clean, they forgive small things for months. Testing is about controlling the first impression of something you have been paid five figures for.

It is also where the retainer gets sold without saying a word. A client who watches a system handle a messy edge case properly understands why it is worth paying to keep it running.

## Three jobs, end to end

- **The clean job.** Everything present, everything normal. It goes all the way through with no human stepping in except where the plan says they do.

- **The messy job.** A missing field, a wrong format, a duplicate of a job already in the system. Each lands where the plan says, flagged for a person, not silently dropped and not silently guessed.

- **The job from the client's seat.** On a phone, logged in as the role that will actually use it, not as you with full access. Half of what you find will be about what that person can and cannot see.

Go back and forth with Jarvis the same way you built it. Input, what happened, what should have happened. One fix per message. Rerun the job from the start after every fix, because a fix in step four can break step two.

## Jarvis writes the checklist from the plan

```
From this build plan [paste] and this build log [paste], write me an end to end test checklist. Three test jobs: one clean, one with the edge cases from the plan's edge case section, and one run as the [client role] user on a phone. For each job list every step from first input to final output, what I should see at each step, and what counts as a fail. Add the human steps from the plan as explicit checks that a person is actually asked. Format it as a checklist I can tick, with a column for the result and a column for notes. Plain language, no technical terms the client would not understand, because I am going to show them this.

```

## Keep the log

Write down what you ran, what failed, what you fixed, and that it passed on the rerun. Date it. It proves to you the system is ready, it becomes the first page of the handover so the client sees the edge cases were thought about, and it is the raw material for your case study.

> NOTE: If a developer built it, this still applies to you. Do not accept the final milestone on their word. Run the three jobs yourself, on the recording or the test system, and release payment when they pass.

## Then show the client, a little

Once the three jobs pass clean, send the client a short screen recording of the clean job going through, with one line: "Here is a job going through your system start to finish. Handover is booked for [date] and I will walk you through it then." No login yet. No poking at it unsupervised. They get their hands on it at handover, with you beside them.

Three passes from the start, dated, in the log. Then book the handover.

### m7.6 Onboard, Hand Over, Get Paid

*As far as the client is concerned, the experience is the product. This is also where referrals get made or lost.*

**They leave with:** A client onboarded, trained, signed off and invoiced.

The system works. Now it has to work for them, run by their people, with their real data. Handover is a process, not a login sent by email. Done properly it is one call and a week of light contact. Done badly it is a month of "quick questions" and a client who never quite trusts the thing they paid for.

## Before you book the call

- **The test log is clean.** Three end to end passes, dated. If a developer built it, you ran them yourself.

- **The client has done their part.** Access to the live tools, the real records to load, the names of the people who will use it. The unknowns list is empty.

- **You know who is running the technical side.** If you are not technical, the person who built it or someone from your bench is on the call flicking the switches while you run the conversation. You own the relationship. They own the switches.

> NOTE: Nobody should do their first handover alone. Bring it to the weekly strategy session, or ask me for someone from the developer list.

## The handover call

An hour, on a screen share, with the owner and whoever uses it day to day.

- **Remind them what they bought and why.** Two minutes. The problem in their words, the number it was costing them, what this system does about it.

- **Walk the clean job through, live.** Say what is happening at each step and who gets told. Then the messy job, so they see an edge case flagged to a person instead of disappearing. This is the single most trust building thing you will do.

- **Go live, with them watching.** Load the first real records. Pick one real job that is in their business right now and push it through. Fix nothing on the fly. If something is wrong, note it and say when it will be fixed.

- **Train the people who will use it.** Each role does their part once, themselves, while you watch. A person who has clicked the button will click it again. A person who watched you click it will message you on Monday.

- **Sign off and the support window.** Go through the acceptance tests on screen, tick them together, and ask the owner to confirm in writing that the build is delivered as scoped.

> SAY THIS: That is everything we scoped, built and tested. You have just watched a real job go through it and your team has run their parts. I am going to send you this checklist with what we ticked, and I need one line back from you confirming it is delivered as agreed. From today you have thirty days of support included: anything that does not behave the way we showed you, send it to me and it gets fixed, no charge. On day thirty we have a short call about keeping it running.

## Sign off, then invoice

The final invoice goes out the day you get the written sign off, not before. Send it with the ticked checklist and the test log attached, so the invoice is a record of what was delivered rather than a request for money. Clients who can see what they got pay faster. If a developer built it, release their final milestone the same day, against the same checklist.

One short document, sent the same day, written by Jarvis from the plan and the logs.

### m7.7 Support, Retainer and Raising the Price

*The install was the start. Everything after it is margin, proof and a higher price next time.*

**They leave with:** A retainer offered, a case study drafted, your next price written down.

You have been paid for the build. Three things in the next thirty days decide whether that was one deal or the start of a business: support it properly, turn it into a monthly retainer, and turn the result into the reason the next client pays more. None of them is hard. All of them get skipped by people already chasing the next sale.

![Thirty days, one call, then the most profitable money in the deal.](/data/img/m7-first-thirty-days.svg)

## The thirty day support window

Thirty days from sign off, and defined. Included: anything that does not behave the way it was shown at handover, questions from the people using it, small wording changes. Not included: new modules, new integrations, anything on the follow on list. Those are change requests with a price, said warmly the moment one comes up. Reply inside a working day, fix inside two for anything that stops a job moving, and keep every request in the deal record.

## What the retainer is

Systems do not run themselves. Tools update, a login expires, a new hire needs training. The retainer is the monthly fee that keeps the system working and keeps you as the person they call. It is usually the most profitable money in the deal, because the work is light and it adds up across clients.

| The retainer covers | In practice | 

| Running costs | Platform, automation seats, AI usage, passed through with a margin. If it costs $300 a month to run, bill $400 to $450. | 

| Monitoring | A weekly check that jobs are moving and nothing has silently stopped. Jarvis runs most of it. | 

| Small changes | A fixed allowance each month. Anything bigger is quoted. | 

| Support and training | Same response times as the support window, plus onboarding new staff. | 

| A monthly report | One page: jobs through the system, time saved, what was flagged, what changed. Your case study, written for you every month. | 

One flat monthly figure that covers the running costs with margin plus your time, simple enough to say in one sentence. Bring it to the group session and we pressure test it.

## How to offer it on the day thirty call

You booked this call at handover, so it is expected. Ten minutes. Open with the month's numbers, then offer the retainer as the way to keep what they have.

> SAY THIS: Thirty days in. [Number] jobs have gone through the system, [number] were flagged for your team and cleared, and you have not had to chase a quote by hand since we went live. Two things I want to put to you. First, the system needs someone keeping it running. The tools it sits on change, your team changes, and it costs [amount] a month in platforms to run. Second, you have already asked for [the follow on items], which I noted and did not build for free. The retainer is [amount] a month. It covers the running costs, me watching it weekly, small changes as you need them, support for your team, and a one page report every month. The follow on work gets quoted separately when you want it. Want me to send that over so it starts from the first?

If they say not now, the running costs still have to be paid by someone. Transfer the accounts to them cleanly, leave the door open, set a reminder in Jarvis for sixty days. A good number come back the first time something breaks.

## Write the case study now

The test log, the build log and the first monthly report are a case study that has not been written yet. Write it in the first month while the numbers are fresh and the client is pleased, and get their written permission before anything goes on a page, an ad or a call.

```
Write a one page case study from the [client] deal record: the problem in the client's words from the call one brief, what it was costing them, what we built in plain language, the before and after of how a job moves, and the results from the first monthly report with the actual numbers from the system. Separate time saved from money saved and keep revenue claims out unless the client gave us a figure. Mark any number that is an estimate. Write it so a similar business owner would recognise themselves in the first paragraph. Then write a two sentence version I can say on a call. Do not publish anything; I will get the client's written permission first.

```

## Raise the price

Your next price is set by your last result. Before this deal you were selling a promise. After it you are selling a thing that exists, with a number next to it. The build is still priced against what the problem costs, but your range moves up, and the proposal now opens with proof.

- Put the two sentence case study into call one, right after you have found the pain: "we did this for a [similar business] last month."

- Add the retainer to every proposal from now on as a standard line.

- Decide the next price today, write it in your offer document, and tell the group session so someone holds you to it.

> EXAMPLE: Made up numbers. First quoting system, $9,000, no retainer. Day thirty you offer $600 a month and they take it. Your next proposal opens with quotes going out the same day instead of in three, prices the build at $14,000, and includes the retainer from the start. Same work. One proof point.

Offer the retainer, draft the case study, write the new price down and tell someone.
