# Sales proposal for call two

Working draft updated 31 October 2026 from Blake's corrections. The proposal supports a buying decision on a live sales call. It must remind the buyer of their problem and its cost, explain what will be built to solve it, show the proposed workflow, state delivery and price, then ask for the next decision. It is not a place to restart market research or explain the agency generally.

## Inputs before drafting

Read the discovery notes, offer version and technical scope. Establish the buyer's actual problems, recent examples, current workaround, volume, time/cost and desired outcome. Confirm what their existing tools already do and the remaining implementation gap. Read [the private input checklist](assets/call-two/CALL-ONE-INPUTS.md) when collecting these inputs.

For the proposed work, establish included workflows, integrations, users, migration, human approvals, important exceptions, deliverables, dependencies and ownership. Use the scoped timeline and approved build/support/tool costs, tax treatment, payment stages and any approved guarantee. Never import another client's price, numbers, proof or timeline.

Save the source/version and status of important facts in a private brief. A fictional worked example is teaching material, not client proof. If a cost baseline, fee or delivery commitment is missing, produce an internal review draft and list the missing decisions privately. Do not fill the gaps with plausible numbers or imply it is ready to issue.

Identify the sales stage. When this is the whole project proposed after call one, write as a new build. Existing development records can inform feasibility but must not turn the proposal into a progress report.

## Reusable client structure

Use [the master template](assets/call-two/PROPOSAL-TEMPLATE.md). Default to eight short pages/sections, with one purpose per page. Combine sections only when it improves the actual deal. Use simple headings and concrete statements the closer can read aloud.

1. **[Project] proposal.** Who it is for, what we propose building, which problem it addresses and the outcome agreed on call one. About two short paragraphs.
2. **Problems.** Two to four specific problems in the buyer's language: what happens, what staff currently do, and the consequence. Use real incidents where supplied, never invented quotes.
3. **Current cost.** One transparent buyer-confirmed calculation with units and period. Show the cost before showing the solution. Separate current cost, capacity released, cash savings and additional contribution. Allow for remaining staff effort and avoid overlapping benefits. Unknown numbers remain internal missing inputs.
4. **Proposed build out.** Three to five included parts, each mapped directly to a problem. State what changes in their working day, the important scope boundary, integrations and what people still approve. No inventory of impressive-sounding agents.
5. **How it will work.** Show one job: input → system action → human approval/exception → useful output. Use a relevant screen or short demonstration. Identify sample data/prototypes plainly. Read [demo patterns](references/demo-patterns.md) when selecting the example; a visual simulation is not a connected integration.
6. **Timeline.** Agreed stages, dates, review points and the client inputs needed. Include testing, training and handover. Do not insert an arbitrary eight-week or 30-day deadline because another example used one.
7. **Costs.** Approved one-off fee, recurring support, incremental tools/usage, currency, tax treatment and payment milestones. Explain the support boundary and material ownership terms. No unexplained package anchors, fake discounts or invented guarantees. Private proposals may show pricing; public marketing remains subject to Blake's no-price rule.
8. **Next steps.** The specific decision: approve the scoped work and terms, sign the agreement, arrange the initial payment and kickoff. Use the actual process. A draft proposal is not a signed deal or launch approval.

## Presentation

Default to a clean local HTML document for screen sharing, plus separate presenter notes. Honour a requested document/slide format. Simple visual hierarchy, readable text, generous spacing and a restrained brand accent. Avoid slogans, landing-page CTAs, decorative brand stories, persuasive-sounding jargon and repeated summaries. Use charts only when they clarify real costs; use visuals to explain work, not to imply results.

Keep calculators, editing fields, discovery questions, negotiation guidance, margin and drafting instructions out of client-facing pages. Calculate privately from the approved brief and render the figures into the proposal. The buyer should not have to watch the presenter complete a template. If live scenario modelling is explicitly requested, keep it separate and label assumptions.

Use one useful demo. A workflow mock-up must show what the buyer receives, not just animated activity. Offer clear manual controls and a readable static state. Avoid automatic audio or decorative motion. Keep essential scope and terms visible; technical detail can go in an appendix.

## Presenter guide and review

Use [the private presenter guide](assets/call-two/PRESENTER-NOTES.md) for what to confirm, where to pause and how to ask for a decision. Keep it separate from the shared-screen file. Presenting firmly, asking directly for the sale and addressing the actual objection are appropriate. Use only real deadlines, accurate proof and promises the delivery team can honour.

Before delivery, check:

- The buyer can explain the problem, cost, proposed change and exact purchase without translating the copy.
- Every deliverable maps to a problem established on call one; numbers, scope, timeline and terms trace to the brief.
- Calculations use matching periods and units, remaining human work, incremental software costs and no double counting. Capacity value is not cash saved by default.
- No outstanding pricing or delivery term is concealed. Incomplete drafts stay internal.
- Open the rendered document, inspect all sections at presentation and narrow widths, operate the demo and check printable output if supplied. A successful render is functional evidence, not evidence it closes deals.

Deliver the client proposal, private presenter guide and a concise readiness report. Do not publish or send without authorisation. Validate this template with student teach-back and actual buyer calls before calling it proven.
