# Jarvis Proposal Builder

Draft candidate · 25 October 2026. Adapted from Blake's supplied proposal-deck reference. Keep its visual explanation and buyer-specific detail; replace its compulsory 23-section marketing presentation with a concise AI implementation proposal.

## Purpose

Help a buyer decide: “You understand our problem. I can see what changes. I understand what we get, what it requires and how we start.” Sell the business result through a credible mechanism. Do not sell an inventory of agents, prompts or software features.

Default to one recommendation and roughly six short sections, suitable for a 5–10 minute walkthrough. Combine sections for a straightforward service; put procurement or technical detail in an appendix when necessary. This is a length preference, not permission to omit terms a buyer needs. Preserve an expressly requested format.

### Buyer-call read-through mode

When the proposal will be read together live after discovery—especially for a custom CRM, app or multi-stage implementation—use a **scrolling proposal** if requested. This is a working document for the closer and business owner, not a lead-generation landing page or slide deck. Write it so the closer can read it aloud without translating it. Use short, natural headings and everyday words. Cut jargon, repeated summaries, decorative labels and paragraphs that sound like a consultant wrote them for another consultant. Keep the deep technical detail in a clearly optional section. Use a plain document layout with no persistent branded header, section-tab bar, or print control unless the user specifically asks for those controls.

For a smaller owner-led business, use the story progression from Blake's supplied video and template examples: remind them what they want, bring their own problem and consequences back into view, show the solution, then cover investment, rollout and the next step. The video teaches this sequence in an enterprise-sales setting; do not import its scale, research claims, executive language, or optional enterprise chapters into a small-business proposal. The supplied PDF is a bank of examples and optional sections, not a required 14-part outline. Sources: `https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gDYHNhk-gOM&t=333s` and `/Users/blakestephens/Downloads/1ffd032-544-b78-ae3-473ea5ce10d5_Killer_Proposal_Template_Examples.pdf`.

Keep the main read-through to a simple sequence; combine or expand parts only when the deal needs it:

1. **What we’re proposing** — about two short paragraphs: what will be built, why it may fit, what changes, and where people stay in control.
2. **What’s happening today** — the buyer’s own words, real examples and impact. Mark anything not confirmed as a question, not a claimed pain.
3. **Follow one job through it** — show the system from enquiry to delivery with one human decision and one exception. Label mock-ups and synthetic data plainly.
4. **What the team gets** — explain the included system and the flow between its parts in terms the owner uses. Keep accepted scope clear; move field lists and technical architecture out of the main read.
5. **Price and current costs** — follow the solution with the buyer's confirmed cost of the current process, then the approved build fee and ongoing costs. Separate released capacity from cash savings. If the numbers are missing, leave clear blanks and say the quote is not ready.
6. **How we’ll build it** — show sequence, roles, client inputs, checks, timeline and launch approval. Keep technical/security detail available without making it the main story.
7. **What happens next** — ask for one clear decision. Separate agreeing the scope from agreeing to build or launch.

Ethical persuasion means making the trade-offs and the cost of inaction specific enough for the buyer to decide. Do not use shame, fabricated losses, inflated anchors, manufactured urgency or make a buyer feel foolish for declining. The proposal should make “proceed” compelling when the measured case is strong—and make “do not build” an explicit, credible option when it is not.

## Establish the deal before designing

Read the approved offer brief/version, discovery-call notes, solution scope and known delivery capabilities. Reuse these records; do not restart market research. Identify:

- Buyer, decision-maker, current workflow and the problem they actually described.
- Desired outcome, baseline, relevant volumes and current alternatives.
- Proposed system, existing tools, integrations, manual approvals and important exceptions.
- Included work, exclusions, delivery owner, client dependencies and technical-review status.
- Approved commercial terms, support, guarantee and next step, if available.
- Real proof or an existing demonstration, and whether this is private sales material or public content.

Maintain `source/proposal-brief.md` with those inputs, provenance and unresolved decisions. Link the offer and scope versions. Discovery observations, public facts, estimates and approved promises are different. If scope is unknown, prepare a diagnosis/scoping proposal rather than pretending a fixed implementation is ready to quote. Ask only for missing information that changes the commitment; proceed with a review draft where possible.

## The short proposal

### 1. What we are fixing
Use the buyer's business language: where work gets stuck, who deals with it, and why it matters. Lead with the desired business outcome, then a precise explanation. A recognisable incident beats “businesses are losing millions to inefficiency.” A separate ornamental cover is optional.

### 2. What changes, and why this approach
Show a simple before/after workflow. Name what the business receives, where it fits into existing software, what people still approve, and how it differs from the native tool or manual process already available. Include the two or three buyer pains this actually resolves. Do not claim a full department is automated when only one workflow is covered.

### 3. See one job go through it
Use one relevant visual demonstration: request → system action → human decision/exception → useful result. For suitable patterns read [references/demo-patterns.md](references/demo-patterns.md). Show the screen or output a buyer will actually use, not a decorative AI dashboard. Include one important exception, such as missing information, an uncertain match or a duplicate request.

Identify what is real: connected demo, recorded working build, clickable prototype, or illustrative workflow. A simulated animation is not a working integration. Use synthetic data for a mock-up and label it unobtrusively. Never display “live”, completed work or client results unless true. A bounded sample is enough; do not build an entire unpaid production system to make a proposal impressive.

### 4. Why it is worth doing
Use buyer-confirmed figures where possible. Show one understandable calculation, including remaining human effort, recurring costs and the time period. Keep capacity released, reduced cash expense, revenue, contribution and cash collected distinct. Do not add overlapping benefits or imply all time saved becomes payroll savings.

If useful, let the buyer adjust two or three inputs. Label assumptions next to the calculation; a calculator is a scenario, not evidence or a forecast. Missing numbers should become questions for the call, not fabricated ROI. Without approved pricing, show gross operational benefit and explicitly leave net return undetermined. Show actual comparable evidence only when relevant and attributable; omit the testimonial section when there is no proof.

### 5. What we implement and how we launch
Summarise 3–5 concrete deliverables, the most important exclusions, client responsibilities, and milestones: access/sample data → build/configure → test → controlled launch/handover. Use the scoped timeline, not a default 30/60/90-day promise. Show who approves launch and what passing acceptance looks like. State ownership, ongoing support and material tool/licence costs in plain language. Keep deep architecture in an appendix for buyers who need it.

### 6. The decision and next step
State the agreed package, relevant risk reversal and a specific next action: scope review, approved pilot, proposal acceptance or kickoff. Do not invent availability, discounts or urgency. Recommend paid scoping where integration uncertainty genuinely prevents a reliable implementation quote.

Public previews, ads, landing pages and VSLs contain no offer pricing. In a private proposal include only approved commercial terms; show setup, recurring costs, currency, billing period and payment milestones clearly. If pricing has not been approved, keep it as an internal decision and say commercial terms remain to be agreed. Do not automatically convert every offer into one prepaid all-in price or invented strike-through value stack.

For “we work free until the result”, preserve the approved promise and define the result, start point, measurement, work that continues, waived fee, client responsibilities and continuing third-party costs. Do not invent a numerical guarantee. Never put an unlimited promise in the deck and a hidden cap in the agreement. Any proposed limit must be visible and consistent wherever the promise is made. Contract drafting is a separate task; do not auto-insert sole-remedy or enforceability claims.

## Visual direction and format

Use a restrained, polished layout: strong headline hierarchy, generous spacing, readable text and one accent colour aligned to the agency/client brand. Prefer a useful workflow or screen to stock photography. The source's situation → plan → work cards and customer/system/team demonstration are reusable ideas, not mandatory layouts.

One visual focal point per section. One hero demo is usually enough; avoid decorative autoplay, background music, cinematic dividers, eight ad examples or market maps unless directly relevant to this service. Optional details can expand, but material scope and terms must remain visible.

For interactive HTML, provide keyboard-friendly navigation, clear play/reset/step controls, reduced-motion behavior and a useful static fallback. Keep presenter notes separate or deliberately toggled; do not expose internal margin or negotiation notes in the client file. On a shared screen, visible notes are visible to the buyer.

Deliver in the requested format. If none is specified, produce a local HTML proposal with a separate short presenter guide. A PDF is optional when requested or useful for sending; ensure the demo becomes a readable sequence and the document stands alone. Do not publish or send automatically. Skill instructions alone do not provide rendering, hosting or integrations; report unavailable execution plainly.

## Review before delivery

- Read it as the buyer: can they explain the problem, the change and what they are buying after a quick scan?
- Check scope, numbers, timeline and terms against the shared brief. Remove unsupported template claims, previous-client names and arbitrary bonuses.
- Check the main objection: why this instead of their existing software, staff or another provider? Answer with specific work and fit, not “AI is the future.”
- Open the rendered output. Inspect every section at presentation size and a narrow screen. Operate the demo, reset, links and any calculator. Check its zero/boundary inputs and recomputation. Inspect PDF pages separately if exported.
- Separate functional QA from commercial judgment. Record inspected files, actual tests, open decisions and limitations in an internal review note. Do not call the proposal high-converting or buyer-validated based on rendering.

Deliver the proposal and presenter guide, with a brief status note and remaining decisions. After acceptance, pass the agreed scope/version, contacts, dependencies and milestones to client onboarding; do not treat a draft as a signed deal.
