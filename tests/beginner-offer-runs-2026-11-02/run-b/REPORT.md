# Beginner simulation B — outside-idea route

## Decision brief

Help US industrial-supply distributors that repeatedly retype emailed customer purchase orders. AI proposes the fields, fixed rules check known customers/products and duplicate orders, and a reviewer resolves unclear units before approving a CSV draft. The distributor keeps final order release.

Why pay: at enough repeat volume, less retyping can free staff capacity and sometimes reduce actual overtime. The illustration frees 88 hours/month; that is not cash unless avoidable spending is confirmed. A hypothetical $3,500 pilot needs real samples, an import test and a specialist quote before it is sellable.

Marketing offer: “Turn orders from three repeat customers into checked drafts with less retyping, through a proposed four-week pilot.” Proposed refund terms cover a failed agreed acceptance test after one correction period. There is no client-results proof or public price in the six-line card.

**Verdict:** stronger hypothesis for discovery, not validated demand or delivery. The first cleaning-inspection idea was rejected after the supervising agent challenged weak economics and native software competition. This intervention was necessary; the simulated novice did not succeed unaided. The offline demo passes logic checks, but browser inspection was unavailable.

**Status: internally tested fictional demonstration and researched offer hypothesis.** This was an AI simulating a US novice with an office/admin background, no agency/coding experience, 15 hours/week, US$2,000 available and access to bounded technical help. No human learner, prospect, integration or sale was tested. The simulation used the actual offer-menu and six-line offer-creation skills plus the proposed four-lesson teaching journey. It did not edit the course or skills.

## One-page service brief

**Who buys.** An owner or customer-service/operations manager at a US industrial-supply distributor with approximately 10–40 staff and recurring emailed purchase orders. Eligibility depends on substantial repeated volume from a small number of customers, an approved product/unit list, a designated order reviewer and a supported draft import. Staff size is a hypothesis, not a demand score. A company with low volume, no reviewer or adequate existing native intake is a poor fit.

**What happens today.** In a fictional example, Cedar Workshop emails PO-1042 for two “packs” of gloves and ten bolts. A distributor employee opens the attachment, copies customer details and lines into a spreadsheet or ERP, checks codes and units, seeks clarification, then releases the order. A resent PO can be entered twice. Time spent retyping can delay acknowledgment and displace customer work. This workflow is supported at category level by the [Conexiom ADI case](https://conexiom.com/case-studies/adi-global-distribution-helps-improve-sales-effectiveness-with-conexiom); Cedar Workshop and all its figures are invented examples.

**Why pay.** Repeated entry at sufficient volume can consume useful staff time and paid overtime. A draft-preparation system may remove copying, not the need to check meaning, stock or commitments. Cash savings require specific avoidable spend; customer-service capacity can matter even when payroll stays unchanged. Buyer-confirmed numbers must replace the illustration below before a proposal.

**Before → after.** Email/PDF → manual keying and scattered queries becomes email/PDF → AI-proposed structured fields → approved customer/SKU/unit and duplicate checks → reviewer resolves exceptions and checks the source → approved CSV draft → client imports/releases through its existing process. AI is useful for varying document layouts; deterministic rules validate known codes and units. The learner is selling less retyping and controlled drafts, not an autonomous order-taking agent.

**Exact first build.** One client-owned intake workflow, three regular customer PDF/email layouts, English machine-readable PDFs only, a maximum 50 approved SKUs and their units, six header fields and line-level SKU/quantity/unit fields, one duplicate key (customer ID + PO number), one reviewer queue and one agreed CSV schema. Unknown customers/SKUs, missing quantities, unusual units or unreadable files are held. No silent inferred substitutions. Four-week pilot proposed after access, assets and technical sign-off; one documented review process, two staff training sessions and 30 days of bounded fault correction. Capacity assumes at most 1,200 scoped orders/month; actual licence throughput must be quoted.

**Not included.** ERP write integration, inventory allocation, pricing/discount decisions, credit approval, shipping promises, invoice/payment actions, handwritten forms, arbitrary attachment types, multiple companies or all customers. CSV is “import-ready” only after a specialist validates the exact target schema and the client tests it in a sandbox. An unsupported ERP import blocks this version of the offer.

**Client responsibilities.** Supply permissioned samples and current catalogue/unit mappings, choose customer and duplicate identities, provide an import specification and test environment, own the licence and data-processing decisions, answer clarification questions, assign a reviewer and perform the final import/release. Client remains responsible for customer-facing commitments. Requiring these tasks contradicts any marketing claim that they “do nothing.”

**Student/support delivery.** Student maps the workflow, obtains approved rules, compares sample outputs, documents exceptions and trains reviewers. Technical helper validates extraction tooling, access and file boundaries, implements checks and review/export flow, and quotes production support before a contract. The student does not improvise ERP integration. No paid build begins speculatively. The offline demo demonstrates the intended decision sequence only.

## Economics — illustrative private hypotheses, US dollars

No public price is in the six-line offer. These numbers are a screening exercise, not vendor quotes or measured savings.

**Buyer capacity.** Assume 1,200 orders/month within the three layouts. Baseline 7 minutes/order = 140 hours. After implementation: 2 minutes review/order = 40 hours, plus 20% exceptions × 2 extra minutes = 8 hours, plus 4 hours/month oversight = 52 hours. Difference = **88 hours/month capacity**. At a $30 loaded hourly value that is $2,640 of capacity-equivalent value, **not $2,640 cash saved**. Timing every pilot order is required; if review takes five minutes instead, the case weakens sharply.

**Buyer cash.** A possible cash case exists only if the buyer can actually eliminate 30 paid overtime hours at $45 = $1,350/month. Illustrative tools $300/month + optional support $250/month leave **$800/month incremental cash benefit**, provided no additional spending or lost capacity offsets it. A hypothetical $3,500 setup would then recover in about 4.4 months. If no overtime/temp spend is avoidable, cash savings are $0 before costs and recurring cash is **negative $550/month**. Do not present the same 30 hours again as extra benefit on top of the 88 hours. A faster draft does not prove extra sales: an illustrative $20,000 of additional shipped revenue at 20% contribution margin is $4,000 contribution before incremental costs, not $20,000 profit; it is not included in this case because causation and actual orders are unknown.

**Student delivery.** Hypothetical setup fee $3,500. Technical help 10 hours × $75 = $750; test tools $150; contingency $250 = **$1,150 cash provision**. Student labour 36 hours × $25 opportunity cost = $900. Fully costed provision $2,050; contribution $1,450 (41.4% of setup revenue), before selling time, tax and general overhead. Cash remainder before student labour is $2,350, not profit. A refund guarantee could consume all fee revenue while costs remain. The learner’s $2,000 covers the assumed provision with $850 untouched, but there is no actual quote. If specialist cost doubles, margin falls to $700 / 20%. Do not accept on these assumed costs.

36 student hours can fit within four weeks at 15 hours/week, leaving 24 hours for learning, revisions and acquisition. One such build per month produces $3,500 setup revenue, not $20,000/month. Six simultaneous monthly builds are incompatible with this beginner’s time. The exercise does not establish a path to the programme target.

## Strongest alternative and why buy implementation

[Conexiom](https://conexiom.com/case-studies/adi-global-distribution-helps-improve-sales-effectiveness-with-conexiom) is the strongest opened purpose-built native software alternative: it already handles customer PO sales-order automation with ERP deployment. Its cost and small-customer suitability were not verified. Do not claim it is unaffordable. The buyer’s own ERP may also have a sufficient module; their platform is unknown and must be inspected before proposal. If it handles this correctly with ordinary vendor onboarding, recommend that route.

A [Docsumo review-and-export workflow](https://support.docsumo.com/docs/export) is another direct DIY route. Implementation fees are for approved SKU/unit mappings, real document exceptions, duplicate policy, import compatibility, tested review ownership and adoption, not access to “exclusive AI.” This implementation case is stronger than the cleaning branch but still competes with vendor implementation and internal staff. Why a buyer would choose this novice is unresolved; a demonstrated narrow pilot and named technical helper can reduce risk, not create authority. The opened Docsumo PO URL redirected to AP, so it is component evidence, not verified sales-order production suitability.

It retains the niche, problem and workflow above, no public price, no fabricated client proof and a **proposed** bounded done-for-you refund remedy. Four weeks, acceptance tests, the ten-business-day correction period and refund terms are proposed contract hypotheses requiring technical/commercial approval. They are not a claim that this novice has proven delivery speed. Acceptance should cover at least 30 agreed held-out samples, zero silently released duplicates/unknown units, field-by-field reviewer checks and correct approved CSV import in a client test environment; extraction coverage must be measured rather than invented. This local demo does not pass that production test.

## Two-minute teach-back — AI-written script, not a recording

“I’m looking at US industrial-supply distributors that receive lots of repeat customer orders by email. Their team opens each document, copies the details into the order system and checks what the customer meant. That work matters, but repeatedly typing it can delay the queue and use time the team could spend helping customers.

My first service idea was cleaning inspections. I dropped it when I found the software already did the job and the savings did not justify a large fee. This order workflow has a clearer use for AI: reading differently laid-out orders into a draft. It still needs a person to check meaning.

Here is a made-up order. The customer asks for two packs of gloves. The system recognizes the product, but our catalogue sells boxes. It holds the order instead of guessing. The reviewer confirms two boxes, checks the other fields and approves a CSV draft. This other sample repeats an existing PO number, so it cannot be released. Nothing in this demo goes into a real ERP.

For a pilot I would cover only three customer layouts, fifty product codes and one approved output file. The client supplies samples, unit rules and a reviewer. I arrange technical help to test extraction, duplicate checks and the actual import format. Their team still decides prices, stock, credit, shipping and final order release.

The business result is less retyping with controlled drafts. The illustrated eighty-eight hours are possible capacity, not guaranteed cash. I must find out whether overtime is actually avoidable. I have admin experience and a demo, not client results. Before selling, I need their real order samples, timing, software check and a specialist’s fixed scope. If their current tools already solve it, I would not pitch a duplicate build.”

## Honest friction log and result

- The lesson’s service-brief bridge helped explain buyer/workflow/value before six lines. It prevented the marketing card from becoming the only understanding artifact.
- The menu skill explicitly allowed an outside idea. Catalogue ad activity was not used as demand or profitability evidence.
- **Critical commercial failure:** the AI beginner initially treated a real pain and easy demo as a recommended high-ticket service. The supervising agent explicitly challenged this. This run therefore was not fully independent beginner success. Add an explicit “would native software/vendor setup solve this, and is cash/capacity value large enough?” decision before recommendation.
- The corrected PO service has higher potential value but much greater technical and data-mapping risk. “Technical help available” is not a quote, competence proof or unlimited fulfilment. A beginner needs a concrete pre-sale feasibility/cost handoff; a post-sale scoping skill comes too late for pricing this pilot.
- “AI” is justified for document variation, but an OCR/extraction product may be sufficient. A custom app is optional, not the economic reason to buy.
- The six-line skill forces a remedy field. The draft labels all remedy terms proposed; no evidence supports a revenue guarantee. Timeframe and refund particulars still need negotiation rather than automatic publication.
- Research friction mattered: the Docsumo search snippet described an old PO page, while opening it redirected to AP. Recording the redirect prevented a false claim that we inspected the old service page.
- No real novice comprehension, confidence, tool access or ability to operate this workflow was observed. The two-minute teach-back is an AI-written script, not evidence a student can explain it.
