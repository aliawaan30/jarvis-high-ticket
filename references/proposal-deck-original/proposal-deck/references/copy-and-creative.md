## The voice

Formal-polished, warm, decisive, numbers-first. "We", not "I" — it frames the presenter as a team and a partner.
Plain-English metric names ("cost per enquiry", not "CPL"). Spelling of the prospect's country (Australian English in
the template: optimise, colour, programme). Optimistic, never hyped.

**The test for every word: would the presenter say it out loud, comfortably, on a call?** Words read off the client's
website ("concierge", "curated", "bespoke") are not safe just because the client used them — the presenter has to say
them. Short sentences, concrete nouns, the benefit stated as an action:
- "2 evergreen + 2 rotating" → "4 products · 448 ads"
- "they arrive with a brief" → "they arrive qualified"
- "including swapping the advertised stock as your list changes" → "when a villa sells, we swap it for the next one, free"

## Headlines and body copy

- **Headline = a plain fact, then its consequence**, the turn in italics:
  *"Your current ads promote the whole company to everyone. None of them are aimed at Australian buyers for this
  development."* — not
  *"Seven residences, a finished film."*
- **Each point is "X, so Y."** Every line has a reason behind it.
- **No metaphors, fragments, clever contrasts, puns or slogans.** "It's not a poem. It needs to be clear, have a reason
  behind it, make sense." When porting a template, rewrite its stylish lines into plain ones — don't keep them.
- **No build-up or mystique** ("what follows is not a concept…"). State the strategy literally, as numbered steps.
- **Less is more.** Summary cards: a headline and two short sentences. Case study: the case and nothing else. If a
  sentence explains something the reader can already see, delete it.
- **Don't state their situation back as if it were insight.** They live inside their own advantages. A value line must
  tell them something they couldn't have worked out — ideally in money.
- **Never quote or list their brief back to them.** Show you understood by describing their situation in your words.
- **Print their best objection and agree with it** when it's the argument: a panel carrying the objection verbatim
  ("But don't you build the ads around the development anyway?") answered by conceding it completely and showing the
  concession *is* the point. The note tells the presenter: if they raise it first, agree enthusiastically and read the panel.
- **Name the industry-wide objection on the page that already answers it** — one line, never a new slide. The
  rest goes in the presenter's mouth.

## Numbers and claims

- Every figure traces to `source/facts.md` (theirs), the user's real proof (yours) or a dated, linkable source (third
  party). Anything else is a `⟦…⟧` marker until it's real.
- **Conversion assumptions are never yours.** Use a published, citable third-party benchmark as the opening value of a
  slider, and let the prospect set their own. On the call: *"that's what we've done; this is what the market publishes,
  and we've modelled you on theirs, not ours."* If no benchmark exists for your market, make it a slider that opens
  conservatively and say so.
- **Turn an unknown into a control, not a stat.** When the killer line needs a figure nobody has measured, build the
  mechanic, default it conservatively, and let the presenter or the prospect set the input.
- **Stat bands carry volume, value and risk facts only** — never a bare conversion rate (it reads as a guarantee).
- **One arithmetic step.** Any number the presenter must derive out loud has to survive being said, unaided, in about
  twenty seconds. Prefer one division and a concrete unit (100 enquiries, dot grids) over a chain.
- **Regulated industries — check before you write a single ad.** The rules differ by country and change; verify the
  current ones for the prospect's market, and when in doubt leave the claim for the call. Common traps:
  - *Property and investment:* never promise or imply returns, yields, capital growth or "guaranteed" outcomes in ads
    ("returns on request — explained on the call"); no sold-out claims you can't prove; no "best" or "most secure". Some
    countries put housing ads in a special ad category that removes age, postcode and lookalike targeting.
  - *Finance and credit:* special ad categories in many markets; no rates or approvals you can't stand behind.
  - *Health, medical, dental, cosmetic:* in Australia, testimonials and reviews are banned from advertising a regulated
    health service (so "4.9 stars from 310 reviews" can sit in the deck, never in an ad), no before/after images where
    restricted, no "pain-free", no outcomes promised. Meta's personal-attributes policy forbids hooks that assert or
    imply the viewer's health condition ("Missing a tooth?", "Struggling with your weight?") — address the treatment, not
    the person. Lead-form questions about health or finances may be restricted too.
  - Ad treatments that read as testimonials or news (chat screenshots, quote cards, clippings) are the first to cut in
    a regulated vertical.
- Never claim where a competitor's or client's ads run without checking each country in the ad library (see SKILL.md, Step 2).

## Presenter notes

`<div class="note">` inside each slide; hidden until the presenter presses **N**. They are what lets a page stay at
~80 words: the page is for the prospect's eyes, the note is for the presenter's mouth.

A good note has a bold lead-in that says what kind of moment it is, then the actual words:
- **"Say this"** — the exact line in quotes. *"Before you take my word for anything — these are our own numbers,
  straight out of Ads Manager, campaign by campaign."*
- **"Do this live"** — what to click, in what order, and when to stop talking. *"Start on the top dial… then hand them
  the sliders and stay quiet."*
- **"If they ask…"** — the challenge you expect and the answer, including what to do if they question the key assumption.
- Use the prospect's name; let them own decisions (the pace, who takes the call).
Anything that only needs saying once, out loud, belongs here — the full rebuttal, the arithmetic, the secondary
argument, the reasoning behind the price.

## Ad creative

The ad pages *are* the creative deliverable being sold ("everything is already built"). An undesigned image undermines
the whole pitch.

**Every ad is a finished, designed static:** an eyebrow / location tag, a display headline, a price or offer pill, a
feature strip, the brand lockup — composed over the client's real photo, in a realistic feed frame (page name and
avatar, primary text with "See more", the headline row and button, reactions). Never a bare render dropped into a feed
frame. State on the page that these are *the finished files, sized and ready to upload to your own ad account*.

**No two ads on a page may share a layout or an angle.** Look-alike ads read as one idea with the picture swapped — and
operationally they bid against each other in the auction and push costs up. Say that line on the page; it turns a design
note into a strategy point. The template's library of treatments (rotate them; none repeats within a page):
- magazine cover · two products side by side · print set (polaroids) · tile grid with a centre seal · directory sign
- one giant number · picture with a caption card · myth vs fact · a chat/DM exchange · a document/file card
- a price tag · a VIP pass · a calendar/date · an invitation · a key or keycard
- a quiz/poll · a receipt · a lock-screen notification · a news clipping
- half-panel colour block (and mirrored) · full-width bottom bar · big centred question over a scrim · checklist with
  ticks · inset spec panel · typographic card with the photo as a strip · two-column "what everyone sells" vs "what we show you"

**Angles, not just layouts.** Each ad argues a different thing — the price, who's behind it, who runs it, what's
included, the family angle, the paperwork, render vs reality, the membership, the location, "still deciding?" Hooks are
**direct-response callouts, never clever agency lines** — a hook should make it obvious who it's for ("GLAZIERS —
SYDNEY" on a bold strip, then one concrete benefit, then one call to action). "You quote the glass. Now quote the
screen." was rejected: *who is that even calling out?*

**Every ad gets a "Why it works" flip** — one plain reason (the audience, the doubt it answers, why this photo), and its
ad-set chip above it. Give every product in a multi-product engagement equal creative treatment.

**Diagrams are creative too.** Boxes, hairline connectors and text "look like a kid made it on Canva". A diagram gets the
same bar as an ad: photography-led, layered, real depth, real numbers on every element, interactive where it helps.

## Stories and video captions

Stories are 9:16, drawn in container units so one design serves the thumbnail and the full screen. Keep everything
important out of the top ~14% (progress bar and page name) and bottom ~20% (the call-to-action button). Eight stories,
eight native treatments: giant condensed type stack · incoming-call screen · news lower-third · split-flap departure
board · poll sticker · wallet passes · number tiles · notes-app checklist.

Video captions count as layouts: forty-eight tiles all captioned "white box + red highlight" read as one ad. Rotate
native caption styles (outline, karaoke pill, black boxes, serif lower third, typewriter strip, marker scrawl, message
bubbles, headline bar, stickers, subtitles, stacked condensed, question sticker, notification, big first line, circled
word…), never repeated beside, above or within one video's openings.

## Photos

- **Claims about the client's product use the client's own photography**, matched to the line. Stock is only for lines
  about the place or the market in general — and then it must be unmistakably that place and that subject (a property
  claim over an empty field or a generic beach is a fail). Verify every image by eye before assigning it.
- No photo repeats within a page where avoidable; across the deck, vary it. "Stop using the same photos for everything."
- A photo with a person *in the transaction* (a couple looking at the listing on a laptop; the sales team at their
  screens) beats any scenic shot. Put type where the photographer left the frame empty; a one-directional gradient
  behind the type only, never a full-frame scrim.
- Logos: a white version for photos and dark bands, a dark version for light backgrounds, a square avatar for the page
  identity in ad mock-ups. Give a stacked logo room — don't squash it into a slot sized for a wide wordmark.
- Never generate or fake a real, named person's face or a client's team photo.

## The qualifying form

The form is where lead quality is won, and showing it accurately is a differentiator. The standard:
- **Three paginated screens** with a real progress bar: Step 1 of 3 · questions 1–3 · Continue → Step 2 of 3 ·
  questions 4–6 · Continue → Step 3 of 3 · your details (name / mobile / email, prefilled with a tick) · "Review before
  you send" · Confirm & send · privacy line.
- **Six questions** — more is friction, fewer is a soft lead (check the platform allows each one for the vertical). For a product: which product · what it's for · budget (in
  bands, never a free number) · timeframe · funding · familiarity. For a middleman: area · budget · type · purpose ·
  timeframe · funding.
- **Three-stage contact verification, where the user's tools support it** — prefilled by the platform (nothing to mistype); confirmed on screen (the
  higher-intent form type adds the review step); verified by a code to the mobile and a code to the email before the
  enquiry exists (*no code, no enquiry*). Show the payoff as two "verified by code" chips on the enquiry card.
- **Say the cost out loud:** six questions plus codes deliver fewer enquiries than a bare form — "that is the point, and
  it's already priced in" — the budgeted cost per enquiry is the cost of a *verified* one.
- **Only show the code step if the user's tools can really do it.** If they can't, remove it everywhere it appears —
  the film's code screens, the form slide, the "verified by code" chips, and the "Every Number Answers" guarantee — and
  don't promise "every number answers". Confirm the verification step can actually be built on the tools in use (some form/CRM tools have built-in SMS and
  email code verification; a plain "email validation" setting that only checks the format is not the same thing).
- Use the same six questions, word for word, everywhere they appear: the film, the form, the plan, the report.

## Client-facing emails and reports

The weekly-report email on the reporting page shows the voice the prospect will get every Friday:
- Open on the purpose ("Please find this week's report attached"), then lead each paragraph with the number and
  interpret it ("We're at thirty-seven verified enquiries at $25 each — Perth and Brisbane leading…").
- Optimistic, never hyped ("solid", "trending positively"). Decisive ("No changes this week" — not "we might wait").
- Close on cadence (when the next change goes live, when the next review is) and a warm sign-off.
