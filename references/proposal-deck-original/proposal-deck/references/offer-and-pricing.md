# The offer, the price page and the guarantees

The deck exists to make one page land: the package (`#included`). Design it first, then build every other page towards
it. The thinking is Hormozi's *$100M Offers* (make the offer so good they'd feel stupid saying no — raise the dream
outcome and the likelihood, cut the time and the effort, then price it) and Carnegie's *How to Win Friends* (open with
genuine appreciation, frame everything in their interests, let them own the decisions).

## The package page, top to bottom

**One long card**, read as a single scroll. Nothing between the sections but the sections. In order:

1. **01 What you get** — the priced value stack, ending on **Total value**.
2. **02 What is guaranteed** — one hero guarantee, then four named guarantee cards.
3. **03 Your price** — the struck anchor(s), arrows, and one dark price card. Stop.

Why this order: *"we don't want to mention the actual price before we get to it — build suspense, build value, so when
we get to the price it feels like it's worth it."* The stack anchors, the guarantees remove the risk, and only then the
number. Naming the fee early spends the suspense the whole page exists to build.

**The audit that proves it's right:** list every `$` figure in the markup from the top of the card to section 03. Only
*value* (stack line prices, total value), the client's own ad spend, and risk numbers may appear. Your fee must not
appear once before section 03.

Don't redesign this page once a version has been approved — improve its content in place.

## The value stack

- **Group it** (e.g. *The campaign · Your setup and sales system · Sales support · Included in the price*), each group
  with its subtotal.
- **Each line = a bold benefit + one plain explaining line + its price**, right-aligned, tabular figures, descending by
  value within a group. Understandable at a glance — no jargon ("448 live ads, with new designs every month" — not
  "dynamic creative optimisation").
- **The prices must sum exactly** to each subtotal and to the total. Check it in code, and again in the rendered page.
- Price lines at what they'd genuinely cost bought separately. A prospect who prices things for a living will audit an
  inflated rate card, and if one line collapses the anchor goes with it.
- If the user normally charges a setup fee and is waiving it, include it as a line ("Account build and onboarding") — it
  makes "nothing extra to set up" concrete. If they never charge one, don't invent it.
- Where ad spend is inside the price, show it as its own group ("Included in the price · Meta ad spend for the quarter")
  and drive it from the dials (`data-live`), so it always matches the pace page.

## Guarantees

**A promise is not a guarantee.** "50–100 enquiries in your first 30 days" is a promise. **"The First-Hundred
Guarantee** — 100 verified enquiries in your first 30 days. Fall short and we keep running at no fee until you have
them." is a guarantee. The name makes it memorable and quotable on the call; the remedy is what removes the risk.

Every guarantee you write has four parts — four short sentences, each doing defensive work:
1. **The promise**, with a hard number and a hard window.
2. **One make-good, bounded in time** — "we keep running at no further fee for up to a further thirty days".
   Uncapped free work is the real exposure.
3. **The sole-remedy sentence** — "that is your only remedy; no refund, discount or damages arise." Counter-intuitively
   this protects *you*: a bare promise that's missed leaves the client free to claim ordinary damages.
4. **The conditions that keep it alive** — budget maintained at the agreed level, approvals inside two business days,
   the campaign not paused or restricted, no amount overdue. Days lost extend the window.

On the page: **one hero guarantee** (the big one — e.g. "The First Sale Guarantee: a campaign buyer reserves inside your
quarter; if none does, we keep working at no fee until one does — it holds while your team works the programme with
us", with the programme's conditions listed beside it) and **four named cards** in a 2×2, each one line of promise +
one line of remedy. Examples from the template:
- **The First-Hundred Guarantee** — the volume promise, make-good "we keep running at no fee until you have them".
- **Every Number Answers** — every mobile and email confirmed by code; if one reaches you dead on both, it's replaced free.
- **The Six-Day Guarantee** — live within six days of onboarding; every day late is a day added to the quarter, free.
- **Keep Everything** — the ad account, the ads, the CRM setup and every buyer are theirs from day one.

**Where each part lives.** On the slide, a guarantee is short: its name, the promise and the make-good in a line or two
(the hero card lists its conditions beside it, as the template does). The bound on the make-good ("for up to a further
quarter"), the sole-remedy sentence and the full conditions go in the **agreement** — every one of them, every time.
The template's cards say "we keep running at no fee until you have them"; that is the slide wording, and the agreement
is where it gets its limit. If the deck is sent as a PDF with no agreement behind it yet, add one fine-print line under
the cards: "Each make-good runs for up to one further quarter and is the only remedy. Full terms in the agreement."

When the user's own wording breaks a rule here (e.g. they say "we keep running until you have them", uncapped), use
their words on the slide — it's their promise — and put the bound in the agreement, and tell them you did.

Rules:
- **Say what keeps running.** "We keep running at no fee" means *your* fee. If the client pays the ad platform, say on the
  card that their ad spend continues — otherwise the make-good misleads.
- **Guarantee what you control.** Enquiries are yours; bookings, attendance and sales depend on the client's team. If
  you guarantee an outcome they influence (consults booked, calls held), make their part a condition (calls within the
  hour, slots open, budget kept) and put the implied conversion in the presenter note so the presenter knows what the
  promise rests on.
- **Tie it to the planned budget.** The dials let the prospect pick a lower pace on the call; the guarantee is stated
  at the recommended pace and conditioned on "at least $X a month". The note says so, so nobody thinks it moves with the dial.
- **Fewer is fine.** The template shows a hero plus four cards because that deal had them. Use the guarantees the user
  will really stand behind — two good ones beat four padded ones — and list any you've drafted as proposals to confirm.
- **Derive any volume guarantee from the deck's own calculator**, so it is provably deliverable (e.g. 5 a day × 30 days
  × the share that verify ≈ the floor). If the guarantee and the calculator disagree, keep the promise and fix the
  wording collision — never water down a promise to make a slide tidy.
- Controls are not guarantees ("you approve every ad", "ad spend stays on your card", "thirty minutes of your time").
  They go in fine print, not in the four cards.
- Never name a card the same as another panel on the page.
- Never let a guarantee contradict a payment option ("you're not billed until it works" beside a pay-today option).
- Keep the wording a make-good ("we keep running free until you have them"), never "you don't pay" — the make-good
  survives a prepaid client and is the version that goes in the agreement.

## Who pays for the ads

Settle this in intake — it changes the stack, the price card and the maths.
- **Ad spend inside your price** (the template): you pay the platform; the stack carries an "Included in the price · ad
  spend for the quarter" group driven by the dials; the price card shows *fee + ad spend = all in*. Simple for the client,
  one invoice — and you are fronting their media, so price that risk in and get paid before it's spent.
- **Ad spend on the client's own card**: take the ad-spend group out of the stack (or show it as a separate row labelled
  "paid to Meta by you — not part of our price"), show only your fee in the price card, and keep the dials (they still set
  the pace). Check the ladder afterwards: the struck stack total **must still be clearly larger than the price** — if
  removing ad spend leaves the anchor below the price, drop the anchor rather than show a "discount" that goes up.
In `src/script.js`, `CFG.price` and `fee` assume the first model; for the second, set `CFG.price` to the fee alone and
remove the `data-live` ad-spend spans from the price card.

## The price

**The user's own terms come first.** If they always bill monthly in advance, or have a standard retainer, use that
and show the period on every figure (per month *and* the quarter). Everything below is the default for when they don't.

**Default: ONE price, reached by a struck ladder, with a named reason.**

```
Everything above, bought separately · the quarter      $5̶8̶,̶3̶0̶0̶     (struck, large, centred, alone)
                         ↓
Our standard fee for one campaign · the quarter        $2̶0̶,̶0̶0̶0̶
                         ↓
┌──────────────── LAUNCH PRICING ────────────────┐
│  Your investment · the quarter                  │
│  $12,000  our fee  +  $12,000  ad spend         │
│              $24,000  all in, for the quarter   │
│  One quarter, paid on signing                   │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

- **Give the anchor a moment of its own** — the struck total centred, alone, large, with an eyebrow above and one line
  under it. That silence is what makes the price land as relief. (Note: let it sit on screen a beat before speaking.)
- **Only carry a "standard fee" rung if it is genuinely higher than the price** and you'd really charge it.
- **The discount always carries a printed reason** — launch pricing, the last spot this quarter, one of only two
  developments taken this quarter. **And it must be true.** If the price *is* the standard fee, there is no discount:
  drop the "standard fee" rung and the "Launch pricing" tag, anchor on the stack alone, and let the guarantees carry the
  close. A reason to act now then comes from something real — limited capacity you actually have, the client's own launch
  date, a season — never invented scarcity. Keep that reason in the deck and the presenter note, never in the agreement (a "last
  spot" line in a contract becomes a representation).
- **Pick the number the deck recommends, and make every instance agree** — the price card, any saving line, the
  summary, the notes, the PDF.
- **State the period on every price** ("for the quarter", "for the first month").
- **When it's paid.** The default is paid on signing, to get started — simple, normal, nothing to police. The
  alternative is "nothing until it works" (invoiced the moment the first qualified enquiry lands); it needs a
  cancellation fee for a client who stalls before the trigger, drafted as a genuine pre-estimate of the work done, not a
  penalty. If you switch between the two, sweep every "$0 until…" line in the deck and the agreement.
- **Two options** (pay-when-it-works at a higher number vs pay-today at a lower one, the better one flagged
  "Recommended") converts well for some buyers; if you use them, the two cards are the LAST thing before the close,
  "Recommended" points at the better option (give it something extra that costs you nothing — not just a lower price),
  and the risk-free option must not end on a fee. One price is the simpler default.

## Success fees

A success fee is an alignment story ("we only earn again when you do — no sale, no fee"), not another cost. Its base
depends on what the client actually earns:
- **Owner** (they sell their own stock) → a % of the sale price (e.g. 1%), payable on the buyer's first payment under the
  contract of sale — never on an expression of interest or a holding deposit.
- **Middleman** (they earn a commission) → a share of *their* commission (e.g. 20% of gross commission — at a ~5% rate
  that's about the same dollars as 1% of the price), payable when they're paid, with their other income streams
  explicitly excluded. Charging a middleman a % of a price they never receive reads as taxing someone else's revenue.
Put the percentage **inside the price card**, never as grey fine print — volunteering the biggest number in the deal
builds trust and stops the deal dying in the follow-up email. And it must be **said out loud** on the call (put the line
in the presenter note): a term the prospect never heard you say does not go in the agreement.

## Bridging value to price

The value stack total is the cost of *your labour*, not the client's return. Bridge to the price with a **unit cost on
your own deliverable** — one division, fully defensible, said in one breath:
*"$7,000 plus about $2,900 of ad spend on your own card buys 119 screened, verified buyers — roughly $83 each. And you
keep every one of them."* Include the ad spend in the divisor; the honest number is still obviously cheap.

If the prospect sent a prospectus, feasibility study or budget, find the marketing / sales-launch allocation and anchor
to it: *"your prospectus already budgets X for off-plan marketing — this quarter costs Y of it. You're not being asked
for new money; you're being asked what the money you've already allocated should buy."* Mine the same document for any
gate (e.g. a pre-sales threshold before construction) and build the deck's headline and forecast around hitting it.

## What never goes on the price page

- **A revenue projection or cost-of-delay figure** — stripped of the sliders that produce it, it reads as a promise.
  Their revenue lives on the forecast page, where they drive the sliders. The price page carries only what you are
  contractually on the hook for.
- The reasoning behind the price ("it only covers our setup cost", "your agency charges five times this"). That's the
  presenter's talk track — the note, at most. **Exception:** a send-only PDF, where nobody is there to say it; then the
  reasoning goes on the card, plainly.
- A second statement of anything already said. Every repeated sentence teaches the reader to skip the next one.
- On the other side: **your fee, your cut or your cost never appears on the client's forecast page.**

## Next steps and the close

The current default ends the deck on the price card. If a closing step is wanted, keep it soft — the presenter should
never feel they're pushing:
**Today · your green light** (the agreement — "we can read it together, or we'll send it over") → **This week · book the
onboarding call** → **Within <your onboarding promise, e.g. six days> · the campaign is live** → **The same week ·
your first enquiry.** Use the same "live within" figure as your guarantee and the rest of the deck.
No price or "$0" column, no "all we need is your signature", no cost-of-delay guilt. The ask is always "a yes, and the
onboarding call booked."

For a client with more to sell than this campaign covers, one line turns the decision from "should I spend $X on this"
into "should I test the system that could run my whole book": *"And this is only where it starts — the buyers you
verify this quarter are an audience you own, and they'll point at whatever you list next."*

## Agreements

The agreement is a closing tool, not a legal artefact. Build it as a separate document after the call (it is not part
of the template) and get it reviewed by a lawyer in your jurisdiction. What has worked:
- **Four pages maximum, "we" and "you"**, headings as plain statements (*What we do · What you do · What it costs · When
  you pay · What we guarantee · Who owns what · How long it runs, and how it ends · The legal bits, kept short*). Page 1
  carries "The deal, in eight lines". Add a precedence sentence under any summary ("the numbered clauses set the full
  terms").
- **Contract only what was said.** If the presenter never raised a term out loud — even if it was on a slide — it stays
  out. Ask the presenter what they actually told the prospect, and reconcile the document to their words.
- **Commit to the principle, never the mechanism.** Write what the client owes and who bears a cost; leave *how* it's
  operationally done to "a method the parties agree before launch". A specific mechanism in writing becomes an
  obligation before you know it's possible.
- **Guarantees** carry their make-good, the sole-remedy sentence and the conditions (above).
- Avoid absolute "non-refundable for any reason" and blanket no-set-off clauses — against a small business they can be
  unfair contract terms (in Australia, the ACL unfair-contract-terms regime). Carve out your own unremedied breach.
- Any client indemnity gets a proportionate-fault carve-out.
- A success fee tied to a sale should be characterised as consideration for advertising services, not a commission for
  introducing buyers (agent-licensing law can bite otherwise).
- Covering emails are pre-contractual representations — never state a conditional guarantee unconditionally in one.
- Tax: state amounts and how tax applies correctly for your registration status (e.g. in Australia, don't write "GST
  included" if you aren't registered; draft as exclusive with a gross-up if it becomes payable).
