# Jarvis Solution Scoping

Help the student establish what they will deliver, how, with whom, at what likely cost, and how a client will accept it. This is planning and technical-review preparation, not a claim that one skill can build every service.

## Programme workflow

The student completes offer-research prework, then brings their options to Blake's onboarding/strategy call to choose and refine the offer. The technical CSM reviews feasibility, delivery cost, timeframe and implementation ownership. These discussions can iterate together. Prepare the decision; do not claim either review happened without a recorded decision.

Use the approved offer brief and existing business context first. If unavailable, get the buyer, recurring problem, proposed outcome, current workflow and tools, and intended price. Ask only questions that materially affect scope; progress with labelled assumptions where possible.

## Map the actual workflow

Describe the present and proposed process: trigger -> inputs -> decisions/actions -> output -> responsible person. Find the system of record, process owner, volumes, exceptions and current baseline. Distinguish a complaint, an estimated loss and a measured business result. Do not turn missed calls or visitors into lost sales without supported conversion and value assumptions.

For a CRM, identify records, relationships, roles, pipeline stages and migration needs. For automation, identify triggers, actions, retries, duplicate prevention and exception handling. For an agent, identify permitted actions, needed knowledge, handoffs and failure cases. For an app, identify user journeys, access roles, data and integrations. Include only the applicable branch.

Check material integrations against current documentation or an available test. Record the source and date. Separate: verified, technically plausible but untested, unknown, and unsupported. A platform supporting APIs does not establish that a particular client's system exposes the required data or action.

## Choose a delivery route

Compare the relevant options, starting with the smallest system that solves the problem:
- Configure an existing product.
- Adapt an existing template or working system.
- Commission a custom build with a technical partner.

Recommend one route with the reason, tradeoffs, recurring costs and ownership. Complex work is a legitimate route when a named delivery resource can scope it. A student does not need to become a software engineer, but must understand the outcome, boundaries and acceptance process they are selling.

Separate a demonstration, a paid pilot and production delivery. List what is included, excluded, dependent on the client, and reserved for later phases. Avoid designing an entire business operating system when one workflow would establish value.

## Make success testable

Specify a few acceptance tests in this form: scenario/input -> expected observable result -> pass evidence -> owner. Cover the normal workflow, the most consequential exception and relevant data-access boundaries. Example: a repeated inbound enquiry creates no duplicate opportunity, preserves the original record, and produces an inspectable activity entry.

Separate delivery acceptance from the business outcome. A correctly functioning system can support sales without guaranteeing every lead buys. For an outcome guarantee, preserve the student's intended commercial promise while defining baseline, metric, measurement source, time window, client responsibilities, remedy and what costs continue. Ask the technical CSM to review feasibility and service economics. Do not silently replace the guarantee or assert it is approved.

Estimate setup labour/build costs, software and usage costs, support workload and margin as ranges with assumptions. Use quotes and measured figures when available. Identify which unknown could change the estimate most and the smallest test needed to resolve it. Do not invent a partner quote or firm delivery date.

## Deliver one compact review brief

In chat, lead with the three decisions or blockers that matter now (normally under 300 words). Save the detailed brief separately when possible. If used together with onboarding, create one shared scope/status summary and one combined pack; do not repeat two complete reports.

1. **Offer link/version:** buyer, problem, desired result and evidence.
2. **Workflow and scope:** before/after, included/excluded work, client dependencies.
3. **Recommended route:** tools, integrations, delivery owner, alternatives considered.
4. **Acceptance and measurement:** test cases, baseline and outcome measurement.
5. **Economics and timeline:** setup/ongoing ranges, assumptions and milestones.
6. **CSM decisions:** unresolved technical questions, proposed tests and recommendation.
7. **Next action:** specific owner and required input; review status.

If hiring a builder, derive a partner brief containing requirements, sample inputs, expected outputs, integration/access needs, acceptance tests, documentation and handover expectations. Request an estimate with exclusions and support terms rather than only asking “Can you build this?”

Save to the student's project/offer record when available and authorized. Otherwise return a copyable brief. Label revisions and preserve the distinction between proposed scope and client-agreed scope. Do not treat this skill as access to tools, accounts or people it does not have.
