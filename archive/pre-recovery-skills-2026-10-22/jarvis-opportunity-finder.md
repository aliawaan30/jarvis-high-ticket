# Jarvis Opportunity Finder
Student-track draft v2. Advanced custom implementation research remains separate in jarvis-offer-discovery.

## Objective
Find a real recurring business problem that is worth solving, simple enough for this student to explain and demonstrate, and feasible to deliver repeatedly with available support. Student familiarity is a useful advantage, not evidence of demand. Simpler does not mean trivial, cheap, novel or limited to voice receptionists.

## Context and breadth
Use target geography, experience, relationships, sales comfort, time, budget and technical support. If missing, ask only material questions or state assumptions when asked to proceed. Explore about six contrasting problem/service/buyer combinations before deepening up to two that merit further attention. Adapt breadth to the request and research budget; do not fill finalist slots with weak options. Start with observed implementations and buyer problems; do not invent an obscure niche and justify it afterwards.
Possible directions include quote/proposal preparation, lead follow-up, customer reactivation, document intake, order handling, support and narrow operational CRMs. These are exploration examples, not a mandatory shortlist. Existing competitors can show commercial activity; inspect their delivery and remaining gaps rather than assuming saturation or an empty market.

## Practical selection
Evaluate each opportunity on:
- A buyer who can describe the recurring problem and its consequences.
- One bounded workflow: clear input → useful actions → output → human checks.
- A demo using sample data that a beginner can understand and reproduce.
- An established delivery route with tools/integrations or a scoped developer task.
- A reachable buyer and credible path to a sales conversation.
- A reusable core for the next client.
Separate delivery complexity from buyer value. Multi-department transformations, specialist regulatory decisions and unclear enterprise integrations usually belong in the advanced track. Explain the reason rather than assigning arbitrary difficulty scores. Technical support can extend the student's reach; it does not remove scoping and maintenance work.

## Commercial usefulness, not just ease
Do not promote a low-value configuration task simply because it is easy to teach. A reminder toggle or receipt-upload setup may be worthwhile, but must earn its place through meaningful buyer impact and work the buyer would pay to have handled. Compare at least one bounded multi-step implementation where useful: inputs from an existing system, an AI-assisted action, human exception handling and an operational output. This is a comparison, not a requirement to add unnecessary AI or complexity.
If researched options are understandable but commercially weak, say so and widen the search instead of naming the least-bad option a recommended business. Keep research preparation separate from a commercial recommendation. Explain the minimum buyer conditions that would make each option worthwhile, using assumptions visibly where needed. No price floor, invented ROI or promise that every simple system supports a high-ticket fee.

## Automation and operating workload
Distinguish what software does automatically, what AI drafts or classifies, what the client reviews and what the student must do every week. Do not disguise a manually staffed administration service as an automation business. A managed service is valid if requested and economically workable; identify it explicitly and check the student's hours/coverage. Compare vendor onboarding and native features with the actual integration, adoption or exception work left for the student. A missing custom feature is not necessary, but meaningful work worth buying is.

## Evidence and economics
Read relevant current primary pages, implementation cases, public offers and buyer accounts. Record exact links and distinguish vendor claims, real purchases, hypothetical examples and your inference. Partial reads are fine if they support the claim; a search snippet or downloaded unread page is not verification. Verify incumbent capabilities before claiming a gap. Explain why a business would pay for setup, integration, adoption or ongoing operation even when software exists.
For finalists show one understandable value model using actual buyer inputs or explicit assumptions. Separate revenue, contribution, labour capacity and cash savings. Include ongoing human work and required software tiers. Never invent a price, market count, guarantee or tool capability just to complete the template. Qualification thresholds use conservative inputs; missing material costs mean the quote remains unscoped. Broad sector counts are not eligible buyer counts.

## Output
Use two layers. The initial student-facing answer should normally fit about 500 words: a compact service menu, up to two short cards and one next action. This is an orientation, not the full research report. Save or place detailed calculations, source ledger and technical questions separately. Do not omit material unknowns to meet a word count.
Label each finalist: research lead, discovery-ready hypothesis, or scoped offer. Desk research alone does not establish the last status. Explain why it merits the next step; absence of an independently published consultant invoice is not proof implementation demand is absent. Do not present buyer validation as already completed. A bounded run may end with no suitable finalist and a specific next research direction rather than a forced recommendation.

Each concise card should cover:
1. We help [specific business type] solve [recurring problem] with [plain-English system].
2. Before and after, including a concrete illustrative job/customer.
3. What we build/connect and what staff still do.
4. Why pay us; observed examples and alternatives with links.
5. How we demonstrate it, and the first delivery/technical review needed.
6. Value model, unknown costs and pricing status.
7. Who buys, how to find them, and the recommended first acquisition test.
8. What could make this fail and what the strategy call must decide.
For the visible cards use plain sentences: who buys, before/after, what is automated versus manual, why pay us, evidence status and the next check. Keep the eight items above available in the detailed brief rather than repeating eight dense sections per option. Include one decisive source link per finalist and distinguish public offer from customer deployment. Label unexplored options as ideas, not researched rejections. Do not force a winner if evidence is weak.

## Student understanding and handoff
Give the student a short teach-back exercise: explain the problem, cost, workflow, demonstration and delivery in their own words. Clarify gaps before turning it into sales copy; confidence should come from understanding and evidence, not exaggerated claims.
The strategy call selects one niche + problem + system. Technical review confirms scope and delivery economics. Maintain one approved offer brief for landing pages, ads, targeted outreach and sales. The targeted-outbound workflow can then find suitable accounts; discovery research alone does not send messages or claim a working automation.
